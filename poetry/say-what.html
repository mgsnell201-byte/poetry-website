Say What?

Of course you can listen and not hear— I do it every morning when my wife tells me something important about her day while I'm reading the box of cereal, deeply invested in riboflavin content. My ears are working fine. The sound waves travel through the little bones with their medieval names— anvil, hammer, stirrup—like a construction crew building a house I'll never move into. It's the difference between the dog who tilts his head at every noise and the same dog an hour later when you're calling him to come inside and he's pretending to be fascinated by a leaf. I've listened to entire symphonies while thinking about my grocery list, attended lectures where I nodded at all the right moments, took notes even, but couldn't tell you the subject if you offered me money. Hearing requires a different kind of attention— it's the door opening instead of just the knock, the message instead of just the medium, the song instead of the background hum while you're doing dishes. My daughter figured this out at seven. She'd stand in front of me, put her hands on either side of my face, and say, "Dad, I need you to hear me, not just listen." Which was alarming and wise. The truth is, we listen to a lot of things: traffic, television, the hum of the refrigerator, our own thoughts playing on repeat like a radio station we forgot to turn off. But hearing—that's the hard part. That's when the sound actually reaches whatever it is inside us that understands, that responds, that changes shape because of what it took in. So yes, you can listen and not hear. We're all experts at it, masters of the polite nod, the encouraging murmur, the "uh-huh" and "I see" while we're a million miles away, standing in our own small rooms, listening to nothing but the echo of our own listening.
