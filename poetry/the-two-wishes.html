The Two Wishes

Have you noticed how the wren carries nothing in her beak she does not intend to give away? This is the story of a king who trapped a small green man among the ferns and demanded everything— the gold, the glittering hoard, the whole shining promise at the rainbow's end. And he got it. He bought a castle made of marble. By morning it was powder on his hands. He bought a ship with sails of silk. By evening it was silt washing against the harbor stones. He bought orchards, bought rivers, bought the Sunday bells right out of the steeple— and each became dust, became ash, became the soft grey nothing that a closed fist always holds. Meanwhile, in the next valley, a shepherd girl was kneeling in wet grass, breaking her one loaf of brown bread and offering half to a stranger no bigger than a rowan branch, and her tin cup— battered, dented, ordinary as rain— filled again. Milk in the morning. Clear water at noon. Warm broth when the thrush sang its last song into the darkening hills. It is still filling. The king closed his hands around the world and the world sifted through. The shepherd opened hers and something ordinary and astonishing rose up like a spring from the deep earth, asking only to be poured.
