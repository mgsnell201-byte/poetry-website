Poem · No. 196

Unashamed

This morning I was thinking about my neighbor who waters his roses every evening at six, how we wave to each other across the fence, how neither of us mentions the cross hanging in my kitchen window or doesn't hang in his. And I want to say this clearly: I am not ashamed. I believe in God, in Christ, in something larger than the circumference of my own understanding. I say this the way I'd tell you my name, not as a challenge, not as an invitation to agree with me, but as a fact about who I am, like having blue eyes or preferring coffee to tea. The truth is, I pray every morning, sometimes on my knees, sometimes standing at the sink with my hands in dishwater, and I'm not embarrassed by this anymore than I'm embarrassed by loving my wife or crying at movies about dogs. But here's the thing—and this matters— my believing doesn't require your believing. The sun comes up whether you notice it or not, and my faith works the same way, quietly persistent, not needing your validation or your conversion to be real. I've learned to carry it openly but softly, the way you'd carry bread to a friend— offering, not insisting, leaving it wrapped on their doorstep if they're not home, never once suggesting they've failed by not being hungry at the same time I am. My grandmother used to say grace at every meal, her voice rising like a hymn, and I do the same when I'm alone, but when friends come over who don't believe, I simply pass the potatoes, grateful they're here. I won't hide who I am, but I won't weaponize it either. What I'm trying to say is this: I am unashamed of the cross in my window, unashamed of the Bible on my nightstand, unashamed to say I believe in salvation, in grace, in something holy moving through the ordinary hours of an ordinary life. But I am equally committed to this: giving you the room to believe differently, to find your own way or no way at all, to stand in your truth while I stand in mine, the space between us not a battlefield but a bridge we can both walk across without abandoning what we carry. What saddens me is not that we disagree, but that somewhere we were taught belief must be either hidden or hostile, whispered in shame or shouted in judgment, when maybe it could be something else— present, honest, unapologetic, and still kind enough to let you be. So I'll keep my cross in the kitchen where you can see it, keep waving to my neighbor at six, keep believing in something vast and loving that holds all of this—the roses, the fence, the waving, your doubt, my faith— and I'll keep thinking that maybe, just maybe, there's room enough in this universe for both of us to be right about what matters most, even if we never agree on what that is.
