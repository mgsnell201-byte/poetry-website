Huh?

I was walking past the post office on Thursday when I heard a woman say to someone on her phone— you don’t know what you don’t know but you need to know that you don’t know— and I stopped right there on the sidewalk like a wind‑up toy that had wound down. Huh, I thought, and then again—huh— the sound of a mind catching itself mid‑stride. It was one of those sentences that eats its own tail, a snake made of appetite, the kind of thing you’d find in a fortune cookie written by a philosophy professor having a bad day. I wanted to chase her, ask her to say it again slowly, etch it on my hand, but she had already turned the corner, taking her koan with her like a purse clutched tight. You know how sometimes you open the refrigerator and forget what you were looking for, so you stand there in the cold light hoping the mustard or the milk will remind you? That’s what this felt like, only bigger— standing in the refrigerator of the universe with the door wide open, light pouring out, and no idea what I came for. My dog doesn’t worry about this. His knowledge is a perfect circle: dinner at six, squirrels are the enemy, the mail carrier is a fiend. He doesn’t lie awake wondering about the things he hasn’t even thought to wonder about. But we do. We’re cursed with this— this ability to know we don’t know, and worse, to suspect there are entire libraries we haven’t found the door to yet, vast, silent shelves of everything we’ll never learn. So I stood there, probably looking foolish, turning the sentence over like a Rubik’s cube, until a kid on a skateboard slid past me and I realized I was blocking traffic, lost in the very thing I was trying to understand— which seemed about right, like the only honest place to be: standing still on the sidewalk, knowing just enough to know I don’t know enough to know what I don’t know— and somehow— somehow, that felt like progress.
