Poem · No. 40

Sock Monkey

From the red heel of the work sock, inverted, the grin emerges—two strands of black yarn drawn through looped cotton in a running stitch, mouth set where the manufacturer's crimson once cushioned an instep. Glass buttons admit the hall's thin wattage, keep it, shallow and round, each eye a lens that takes and stores whatever arrives from the lit rectangle of door. Arranged at the pillow's selvage each evening, the stuffed arms rest on chenille, palms up, fingers curved in their knit skin, ready to receive whatever weight arrives from darkness— the elbow, the cheek, the whole small pressing body that folds against the sewn torso night after night, the same arrangement, the same crease deepening where a chin once tucked. The tail's braid loosens at its tip. The stitched expression holds toward the doorway while the house contracts its joists against frost, while the furnace counts through its ducts. Then one night the covers did not fold back. The buttons held only ceiling plaster. The crimson mouth kept its sewn configuration through the second night, the third, through weeks of unrumpled chenille, a pillow unpressed, a spread drawn smooth. Fresh linen stretched across the ticking. The doorway held its rectangle of hall. The monkey sat against the headboard— yarn fingers open, red mouth sewn shut, button gaze trained on the threshold's edge.
