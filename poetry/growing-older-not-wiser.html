Growing Older, Not Wiser

At seventeen, I thought my father knew everything. At thirty-seven, I realized he'd been winging it for decades, just louder and with more confidence in his wrong answers. Watch the man at the coffee shop who's been coming here for twenty years, still complaining about the same things, still expecting different results. My grandmother hoards forty years of grudges in mason jars, perfectly preserved, pulls them out during family dinners like they're fine wine. The boss who says "we've always done it this way" while the company slowly bleeds money, his experience a pair of blinders he mistakes for vision. Some people don't age like wine— they age like milk left on the counter, growing more sour, more rigid, more certain they're right. Real wisdom whispers, never shouts. It says "I don't know" and means it. It changes its mind when shown new facts instead of building walls around old ones. The smartest words I ever heard came from a kid who asked her teacher, "But what if we're wrong about that too?"— while the adults nodded, uncomfortable. Age gives you stories, not answers. Time gives you chances, not guarantees. And some people spend a lifetime missing every lesson life tries to teach.
