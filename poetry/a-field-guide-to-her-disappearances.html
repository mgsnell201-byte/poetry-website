A Field Guide to Her Disappearances

I've been trying to identify her for weeks now, the way you might try to name a bird you keep glimpsing at the edge of the yard— there for a moment in the hydrangea, then gone before you can get the binoculars focused. Wallflower, I think at first, noting how she positions herself near doorways at gatherings, how she seems to bloom only in the margins, requiring the architecture of a room to hold her upright, to give her some solid surface against which to exist. But then I remember my grandmother's garden, those cheerful little wallflowers with their bright faces, tough as nails, thriving in the cracks of stone, and I realize that's not quite right— there's something more fragile here, something that flinches. Shrinking violet, then. Yes. The name itself a kind of disappearing act. I picture the Victorian lady who named it, probably admiring its modesty, the way it hides its purple face in heart-shaped leaves as if embarrassed by its own beauty, as if existing were a kind of imposition on the world's good manners. But here's what gets me: violets aren't really shrinking at all. They're taking over my lawn as we speak, sending out runners underground, staging a quiet revolution while everyone's distracted by the roses. So maybe she's both, or neither, or maybe I'm the one who needs a better field guide, one that accounts for the complicated taxonomy of human shyness, the way a person can be simultaneously fragile and persistent, hidden and utterly present, the way she can disappear into a corner and somehow still be the most alive thing in the room— a small purple bloom you almost overlook until you crouch down, really look, and realize the whole damn lawn has been turning violet while you weren't paying attention.
