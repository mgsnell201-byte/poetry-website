The Tree Finder

He found it leaning against a dumpster behind the strip mall on Fourth Street— a Charlie Brown tree if there ever was one, three feet of crooked pine with exactly seven branches, the kind of tree that makes you think someone tried to throw Christmas away. But Marcus saw something else entirely. He saw the tree his mother used to decorate with construction paper chains and popcorn strings, back when their apartment had heat and she hummed carols while cooking. So he carried it back to the underpass where the others had already settled in for the night, their sleeping bags arranged like mismatched ornaments beneath the concrete sky, and he planted that sad little tree in a coffee can filled with rocks and snow. No one said anything at first. Tommy kept his eyes on his book, the one he'd been reading by flashlight for three weeks. Rosa pulled her coat tighter. Even the pigeons seemed to hold their breath. Then Marcus started hanging things— a bottle cap that caught the streetlight just right, a blue ribbon from God knows where, somebody's lost mitten, still clean enough, a photograph of a dog that wasn't anyone's dog but could have been. By midnight, others joined in. Rosa contributed a small bell from her purse, the kind that jingles when you move. Tommy tore a page from his book— just one page, the one with the happy ending— and folded it into a star. They didn't sing carols or hold hands or pretend this was the Christmas they'd once known in houses with lawns and families who remembered their names. But they sat in a circle around that Charlie Brown tree, and someone opened a can of soup to share, and Rosa's bell kept ringing in the wind, and for one night, under the hum of traffic above, they were people who had a tree, which made them people who had something, which made them, for a moment, less alone than they'd been yesterday, or might be tomorrow, when Christmas was over and the tree would go back to being trash, but tonight—tonight it was theirs, crooked and impossible and somehow still reaching up toward the light.
