Hard to say Harder to eat

I'm going to say it—fruitcake— there, I said it, the word my brother whispers like a curse when he sees the package arrive, the dreaded F-word of December, and my mother actually shushes him as if it might hear and take offense. It sits on our counter like contraband, dense as a physics problem, dark as a secret, and we circle it the way you'd circle a UFO that landed in your kitchen. My sister claims it's a doorstop with delusions, my father says it's tradition, which is just a nicer word for something you can't get rid of. And me? I'm cutting a slice on Christmas Eve— our one-gift-early ritual—holding this boozy, studded thing on my plate, this edible obscenity, and I honestly cannot tell you if I'm eating it or it's eating me, if this is pleasure or some kind of slow-motion wrestling match with nostalgia. The candied cherries glow like warning lights. My brother said it tasted like a Christmas tree fell into a vat of rum and got fossilized, which isn't wrong, but he had two pieces. My mother says Aunt Rita would be hurt if we didn't try it, even though Aunt Rita has been gone for six years and I'm starting to suspect this is the same fruitcake, just making its annual rounds, My father nods approvingly. Is this love? Is this loathing? Is this just me trying to digest the weight of December itself, all its dense sweetness preserved in a loaf-shaped paradox that tastes like both yes and no, like every complicated feeling I've ever had about things that show up year after year whether we invited them or not— this forbidden word sitting there between us, daring us to take another slice, and somehow, impossibly, we always do.
