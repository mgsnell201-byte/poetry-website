The Grammar of Staying

In the first month of loving you I began setting things down more carefully— the glass on its coaster, the knife in the sink facing inward, the book aligned with the table's edge as if the world might learn from my hands how to keep from breaking. You were making tea. Steam rose between us, a small, invisible river. I watched how the water took the shape of each cup without argument, without defense— how it gave up its heat to whatever it entered. Love is this simple, I thought, and already knew it was not. The kettle reddened the burner into a circle. A circle is a promise that has forgotten where it started. We live in its forgetting. Outside, the oak stood considering its leaves. It has done this for a hundred years— the slow invention of shade, the brief experiments of green, then the months of standing with nothing to show, yet still it remains, rooted, offering the empty shape of its branches to every arrival of light. I do not know how to be that steadfast. In the nights when you turn away in sleep, your back is a small mountain range. I lie awake, measuring its distance, learning the slow art of loving what cannot be crossed by wanting alone. On the counter, a loaf of bread cools. You taught me to wait until its crust no longer burns the hand. If you cut too soon, you said, the inside will tear, the crumb will not yet have become itself. I think of all the words I have hurried from my mouth toward you— how many of them were still dough. I want to keep you. The sentence feels wrong the moment it forms. Stones keep. Mountains keep. What lives refuses that stillness. Even the pebble in my palm moves slowly toward dust. So instead I practice another grammar: not keeping, but staying alongside. Like the creek below the house that does not hold the stones in place, only passes and passes, polishing them into something that can bear being touched. Some days, love is easy to find— your hand reaching for mine without looking, as if the body had grown a new sense, quiet and accurate as a root finding water. Other days, it is a shy animal beneath the house— I hear it moving, I leave a bowl of milk at the threshold, I do not call its name. If there is a secret to not losing you, it is not in holding tighter. It is in learning from the oak, from water, from bread, from the shy animal— to offer what I can: shade, and heat, a crust that yields without collapsing, a bowl set down and left in peace. You pour the tea again. The cups fill and cool between us. Nothing in the room explains why my chest feels newly made, why each small thing— your wrist turning, the faint chip in the rim of the cup— seems both ordinary and unbearable. I do not know how long we are given to attempt this carefulness. I know only that tonight, again, I will smooth the sheet beside you, listening to your breathing as if to a distant weather, and will place my hand, lightly, where your back becomes a hillside— not to claim it, only to feel, for another moment, the moving earth I have been allowed to stand upon.
