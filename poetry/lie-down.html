Lie Down

I am walking through the wet field before the sun has opened its whole hand on the horizon. The timothy bends and holds the last of the fog in its seedheads. My boots sink slightly. The spider has tied her thread between two yarrow stalks, and she herself is smaller than a grain of wild rice, hanging in her own silver. Something moves at the edge of the pines. Not the wind. The brown flank of a doe steps from the shadows and does not lift her listening into the long neck the way they usually do, standing still as a stone while the gnats rise and fall. She turns, and behind her, smaller than a thrown shadow, the fawn follows, and its legs are still learning the earth, and its coat is spotted like light falling through leaves. They cross the open ground with the knowing that lives in the blood, the old agreement between hoof and grass, ear and air. She does not look back, but the fawn, once, turns its dark eye to the trees they are leaving. And the grass is taller there, mixed with clover and vetch, and the stems are heavy and ripe. Into that green abundance they go, and the stalks close over the spotted back, the little ears, until even the white flag has vanished. Tell me, what does she know about the open meadow that I have forgotten? How to step into the clearing and trust the distance? How to lie down in the tall weight of summer and become nothing but the small lungs breathing? How to give the body of what you love to the wide daylight? She knows the fox travels the edge of the wood. She knows danger wears the patience of stone. She knows the field is both door and wall, the grass both breath and held breath. Lie down. Let the grass close over you. The hawk circles. The clover opens.
