For Franz Schrader, Who Mapped the Heights

Have you stood where the fog wraps itself around the mountain, where stone rises through cloud like patience rising through doubt? Franz Schrader knew this— how to read the earth's body, how the Pyrenees held their secrets in folds of rock and ice, how Mont Blanc wore its snow the way wisdom wears silence. He had no diploma, only eyes that could see what mattered— the way a glacier moves, imperceptible as prayer, the way each contour line on his careful maps was a love letter to the world, saying: this matters, this endures, this must be protected and known. Tell me, what does it mean to measure a mountain? Not its height alone, but its being— the fog drifting through pine, the meltwater beginning its long journey down, the osprey circling above where bucolic earth meets the deep blue invitation of sky. He understood what we forget: that inner peace and cosmic harmony are not separate things— that when you stand at the meeting place of river and peak, fog settling into the valley like breath, you are measuring yourself against something that will not yield, something that teaches by simply being what it is. This morning the mountain is half-hidden, half-revealed, and I think of Schrader climbing, his hands cold, his vision clear, drawing what he saw so that we might see it too— not just the rustic beauty, the magnificent indifference of stone, but the truth that to know a place is to love it, and to love it is to fight for its survival. What gift is this, to walk the earth with such attention, to let the mountain enter you the way fog enters the forest, completely, without asking permission? The glaciers are melting. We know this now as he knew it then. But today, on this Christmas morning, let us also know the mountain is still here, still rising, still teaching anyone who will listen that we belong to this world, river deep and mountain high, and it belongs to us.
