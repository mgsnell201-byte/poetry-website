Assume

I've been thinking about how fragile they are, these moments we believe are made of something solid— the last time I carried my daughter upstairs, her weight against my chest like a promise I thought would be there tomorrow. But it wasn't made of stone or steel, that moment. It was made of glass so thin you couldn't see it, couldn't feel the delicate membrane between now and never again. One night I lifted her, and she was light enough. The next week, or the next month—I honestly can't remember—she walked herself to bed, and just like that, the glass shattered so quietly I didn't hear it break. That's the fragile part, I think— not that things end, but how easily they end. How little it takes. One missed phone call becomes the last conversation with your father. One unremarkable Tuesday becomes the final morning you'll wake in the house where you learned to walk. These moments aren't protected by anything. No frame around them saying "Pay attention." No bubble wrap, no warning label: Fragile. Handle with care. They arrive bare and exposed, breakable as an egg balanced on a fingertip, and we move through them like bulls in a china shop, shattering things we can't replace. This morning I held my coffee cup— the one with the chipped handle my son made in third grade, lopsided and perfect— and I thought about how fragile this is too. Not the cup itself, though yes, that too, but this: him still being young enough that his crooked pottery means something, me still being here to drink from it. How many mornings left? I don't know. But I know the number isn't infinite, know that somewhere ahead there's a morning— ordinary, unremarkable—when I'll use this cup for the last time, set it in the sink, and the fragile thread connecting me to his small hands pressing clay will finally snap. And I won't know. That's the thing that undoes me. The fragility isn't just in the moment itself but in our blindness to it, how we can't see the hairline cracks forming, can't feel the structure weakening until after it's already gone. My mother used to call on Sundays. Every Sunday, regular as breathing, until one Sunday she didn't, and that rhythm we'd built over decades, that pattern I thought was permanent as bone— it turned out to be made of spider silk, so delicate that one pulled thread and the whole thing dissolved. I wonder sometimes what I'm breaking right now, what fragile last time I'm living through without knowing. Maybe it's the last autumn I'll rake leaves with my neighbor, the last time this particular dog will rest his head on my knee, the last ordinary evening I'll spend believing I have more ordinary evenings coming. We build our lives thinking we're working with concrete, but everything—everything—is glass. And we don't get to know which parts will shatter today, which will hold another year, another decade. We just keep moving, keep reaching, keep assuming there's time, while the fragile moments accumulate around us like a museum full of priceless things and us, stumbling through in the dark. So sometimes now I try to move more carefully. Not all the time—I'd go mad with that much attention— but sometimes, when I remember, I pick up whatever I'm holding as if it might be the last time I hold it. The coffee cup. The phone. My wife's hand. I try to notice the weight, the temperature, the specific configuration of this moment that will never come again. I try to treat it like what it is: something made of crystal, something that will break, something already breaking even as I hold it.
