The Troglodyte's Cave

Deep within a mossy hole there dwelt a shaggy troglodyte, a hobbledehoy in a caveman's role, all elbows, knees, and appetite. He'd trip on rocks he'd tripped on twice, and bonk his head on the ceiling low, his cave-hair matted, never nice, a bumbling sort, as cavemen go. His home was crammed with odd gubbins — a broken wheel, a chipped stone axe, a pile of bones from ancient dubbins, and forty rocks he called "the facts." He'd organize his precious hoard by size, by smell, by how they gleamed, then trip again, and — oh, how he roared! — watch it scatter, as he screamed. At supper time he'd feel the dread, the rumbling deep down in his gut, for mammoth meat, three days now dead, gave him collywobbles something fierce, tut-tut. He'd clutch his belly, moan and groan, and pace his cave in wobbly rings, this awkward beast, alone, alone, undone by half-cooked, spoiled things. Yet still he lived, this clumsy chap, this troglodyte of gadgets, gas, and grace, a hobbledehoy who'd stumbled, gone to nap, and woken with a smile upon his face. For gubbins broken, guts a-churning, knees that knocked and thoughts that strayed — were simply proof, without much yearning, that even cavemen's flaws can be okay.
