Seventy Christmases

I want to tell you about the boy who could barely walk but got a car anyway—a Murray Jet Flow Drive Woody station wagon, cherry red with wooden side panels painted on, chrome details catching the colored lights from the tree. Christmas morning, 1955, and he's fifteen months old, toddling on unsteady legs across the living room until he sees it there, gleaming like a promise someone made on his behalf to the future. Watch him climb in, all determined limbs and focus, the way toddlers attack the world— no hesitation, no doubt that this vehicle parked between the presents and the pine was meant for him, that his wobbling walk has prepared him for this, his first commute around the coffee table, past the radiator, bumping gently into the armchair where his grandmother sits with her coffee, laughing at this tiny driver who just learned to stay upright and now wants wheels. He doesn't know he's supposed to be amazed, doesn't know this is the first entry in a ledger that will fill with seventy years of torn wrapping paper and ribbon, the encyclopedia of trees—flocked and fresh-cut, artificial and fragrant, the Charlie Brown specimen from that apartment in '73, the one that fell over twice, the towering Douglas fir the year his own kids were small and believed in everything, including him. Let me catalog the Christmases for you: the year his feet first flew, pedals whirring perfect eights into the carpet; the year the living room held a miniature storm, humming on its tracks; the year of socks, a mosaic of small griefs he learned to walk upon; the year he offered a future, small and shining, in a velvet universe; the year dawn found him a weary architect of a miniature world; the year the dog, a furry philosopher, taught them about impermanence and turkey; the year three stores dissolved into the single glow of his daughter’s understanding; the year his name was a forgotten lyric on his mother’s lips, though the carols remained; the year the table’s algebra changed, a silent subtraction; then another; the year the absences themselves became the guests of honor. Here's what nobody tells you about Christmas— it's the same movie playing on a loop, but you keep switching roles: first the child, then the parent, then the one who remembers being both, who stands at the window watching grandchildren track snow across the carpet and thinks about time, that patient engine, how it moves you forward whether you're ready or not, how you keep pedaling even when the wheels are too small to carry you anymore. December 2025, and somewhere in a box marked "fragile" in his mother's handwriting, there's a photograph of a boy in a pedal car, his hands gripping the steering wheel, his face pure concentration, beneath a tree in a house that no longer exists on a street where different families now hang different stockings, tell different stories about what matters most. But here's the thing about that Murray Jet Flow Drive— it was a station wagon, which is to say a vehicle designed to carry people, and if you look closely at that photograph, you can see them all there in the back seat, crowded in like it's the most natural thing in the world: his grandmother with her coffee, his mother adjusting the bow on a package, his father checking the focus on the camera, his wife who doesn't exist yet, his children who are still decades away, his grandchildren who are impossible to imagine, all of them passengers in a car too small to hold anyone but somehow holding everyone. And maybe that's the real gift beneath the tree— not the car itself, but what it carried, not the Christmases we remember but the ones we became, the way love doesn't diminish when we divide it among the living and the dead, the present and the past, the child we were and the person we've become. So when you plug in the lights this December, when you set the table and fold the napkins and wonder why we keep doing this, why we insist on this annual pilgrimage to the same rituals, the same songs, the same hope wrapped in different paper, remember the boy in the pedal car, how he climbed in without asking where it would take him, how he trusted the wheels would turn, how everyone he would ever love was already riding with him, and how, after seventy trips around the sun, he's still there, still driving, still carrying them all.
