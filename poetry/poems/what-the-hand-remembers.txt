What The Hand Remembers

The earth here is the color of old ceremony,

red-brown and iron-warm beneath the fingers,

holding the memory of a thousand plantings

in its layered dark.



She kneels at the field's edge

where the mesa catches the first gold

and presses each seed into its small hollow—

three together, the way it has always been done,

the way her grandmother's hands knew without thinking.



The corn is not yet corn.

It is still a kind of faith,

pale and closed as a held breath,

resting in the rust-colored quiet.



Yet the sky above the canyon

opens blue as water, blue as forgetting—

and the light coming over the ridge

moves across the field the way a hand

moves across something it loves,

slowly, touching everything.



This is what the hands remember

when the mind has grown quiet:

the press and release,

the covering over,

the letting go into the dark

that is not darkness but waiting.



A hawk turns on a thermal far above,

reading the warm air like old script,

and the mesa holds its ancient color—

ochre, sienna, the red that has no other name—

while below, three seeds lie curved together

like a word just spoken,

already becoming something

the sky will have to lean down to see.
