Peace, Considering Its Palette

They tell me peace arrives in amber—
honey held to light, they say,
but I ask: whose honey? Whose light?
The amber that preserved the insect
also killed it. Is this what we mean
when we speak of preservation?

Someone insists on indigo,
the color of evening that suggests depth,
but I want to know: who decided
indigo meant peace and not the approaching dark?
Who gets to name the hour when fighting ends
and call it sacred?

I have been told it comes in silver—
not the bright coin but the tarnished spoon,
and here is my question: is peace
what remains after we've given up,
or what we chose? Is there a difference?
Does surrender by exhaustion count as treaty?

The documents are signed in black ink.
The blood that paid for them is red.
Between these colors, what spectrum
do we inhabit? And who calculated
the exchange rate—three lives for this silence,
ten thousand for that handshake?

My neighbor says peace is brown,
the brown of turned soil, and I wonder
what's buried there. Whose bones
compose the richness we call fertile?
What grows from graves we've agreed
not to mention?

Is peace worth its cost? But who
am I asking—the ones who paid it
or the ones who merely write the check?
The mother who sent her son, or the son?
The border that moved, or the house
that stayed where it was, suddenly foreign?

Tell me: is peace the ochre creek
carrying what the mountain releases,
or is it the mountain itself, still standing
because it can afford to let go?
Is peace a privilege of elevation,
a vista available only to those already above?

Some speak of pale rose, the moth's wing,
beauty that accepts its brevity—
but does peace have the luxury of being brief?
Can the occupied afford to call anything
temporary and still call it peace?
Who gets to choose when the moth unfolds?

What does peace mean in a language
that has no word for it, in a throat
that has forgotten how to shape
the sound of not-afraid?
Can you translate peace into the grammar
of those who've only conjugated war?

I don't want metaphors. I want to know:
is peace boring because it's actual rest,
or because we've stopped paying attention
to who still can't sleep?
Is peace quiet, or have we just
stopped listening for certain voices?

The stone in my palm is grey, patient,
carved by water over centuries—
such soft persistence, such slow work.
But who has centuries? Who gets to wait
for peace to arrive like erosion,
and who needs it now, immediate as breath?

Maybe peace is the color of admission:
we don't know what it looks like
because we've rarely seen it whole.
We've seen pieces—an afternoon, a year,
a single street where, for a moment,
nothing burned. We called that peace

and wondered why it felt
incomplete.
