The Shoulder of the Road

I admire the patience of gravel—

how it keeps to the margin of things,

not road, not ditch,

just the narrow strip where the asphalt frays

and the weeds take over.



You know the place—

where engines sputter and blink their warnings.

Where someone pulls over

to cry about something

they cannot cry about at home.



A family stops to unfold a paper map,

the father leaning on warm metal,

squinting as if the horizon might confess.

Sometimes it’s only one car, hazards ticking,

a blue glow of a screen across a face

lit by messages better left unopened.



The gravel receives it all—

flat tires, urgent calls,

the long exhale of someone

who sits gripping the wheel

and cannot remember

why they started driving in the first place.



We build these in-between places

and call them nothing.

Yet the shoulder holds us

when we can’t hold ourselves.

It keeps the small evidence of our pausing:

a cracked taillight lens,

a receipt gone pale with sun,

a single shoe stiff with rain.



It is always three in the morning there.

The shoulder keeping watch

while we decide

whether to signal and merge

or let the engine idle a little longer—

the world tearing past

at seventy miles an hour,

and us, for once, unmoving.



Even as the backdraft of a passing truck

shudders the glass and rattles the frame,

reminding us, for one violent second,

how thin the metal is between us and the world.
