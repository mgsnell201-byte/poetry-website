White Rabbit, No Apologies

I was sitting in a kitchen with cold coffee,

listening to Grace Slick tell me to follow

a white rabbit down a hole, and I thought:

here is a woman not playing with a full deck,

inviting me to board a plane headed somewhere

between breakfast and nightmare, between

the mushroom and the sky, and making it sound

like the only sensible thing to do.



The song doesn't pretend to make sense.

It arrives certain in its unreasonableness,

so sure of itself that you begin to trust

the rabbit more than you trust the ground.

That's the whole trick. That's how you get people

to believe in impossible things: you sing them

into it, the way a pilot who's lost his compass

still sounds authoritative at the controls.



And I thought about Mary Oliver asking,

"Tell me, what is it you plan to do with your one wild life?"

Well, Grace Slick has an answer:

follow the white rabbit. Take the pills.

Don't ask which direction is down.



Because here's the absurdity nobody mentions—

we're all supposed to be looking for meaning,

for some destination that makes sense,

but the song is telling you the sense

is in the falling itself, in the speed,

in the pill that makes you larger or smaller

depending on which side of the mushroom

you're standing on.



All these years later, the song still works.

Still makes you want to board that plane,

still makes the irrational seem

like the only available flight path.

Though by now we're all used to it—

the pill-taking, the speed, the belief

that if we just keep moving, keep listening,

we'll land somewhere that makes sense.



But we won't. And that, I think,

is exactly what Grace Slick knew.



The rabbit hops on.

The plane is boarding.

Ask the white rabbit what the answer is.
