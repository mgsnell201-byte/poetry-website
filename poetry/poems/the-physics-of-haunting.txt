The Physics of Haunting

It occurred to me this afternoon,
as I was driving down the road,
watching the air shimmering above the asphalt,
that ghosts and heat have something in common—
they both insist on rising.

You can see it if you look closely,
the way warmth distorts the landscape behind it,
bending the mountains, making the ground waver
like something seen through water,
and I thought: Yes, this is how the dead must travel,
hitching rides on thermals,
drifting up from the basement furnace,
from the pilot light on the stove,
from the laptop warming your thighs.

No wonder they congregate in old houses
with their radiators clanking all night,
their ancient boilers groaning in the cellar.
It's not the history they're after,
not the creaky floorboards or the tragic past;
it's simply that these places are warmer,
better for levitation, more conducive
to the general upward drift of the unembodied.

And maybe this explains why we feel them
most often in winter, when we're burning,
everything we can—wood, gas, even
our own calories just to stay alive—
creating all these invisible elevators
for the departed to ride toward the ceiling,
toward the attic, toward whatever comes next.

I like to think of them up there,
my grandmother, my college roommate,
that dog I had as a kid,
all of them floating near the crown molding,
warm and weightless, rising on the very same
principle that lifts a hawk or a balloon,
waiting for us to turn up the thermostat
so they can climb a little higher,
drift a little farther from the cold ground
where we left them.
