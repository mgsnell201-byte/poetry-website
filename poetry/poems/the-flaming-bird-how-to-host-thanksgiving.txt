The Flaming Bird: How to Host Thanksgiving

I want to tell you about the turkey that caught fire,
not just singed or smoking but genuinely ablaze,
a flaming bird my uncle carried through the kitchen
like an Olympic torch, trailing smoke and profanity
while my aunt threw dish towels at him
as if he were a piñata filled with disaster.

This was after the dog ate half the stuffing,
before we discovered it, of course, so we all sat there
complimenting my grandmother on her bold new recipe,
nodding thoughtfully at what turned out to be
Kibbles 'n Bits mixed with sage and someone's good intentions.

You should have seen us pretending to enjoy it,
forkful after forkful of what amounted to
a crime against poultry and basic human decency,
my cousin actually saying, "Is that thyme I'm tasting?"
when what he was tasting was definitely Purina.

The mashed potatoes were actually fine
until my nephew asked why they were sweet,
and we realized my mother had used vanilla extract
instead of milk—an honest mistake if you don't read labels
or believe that all white liquids are interchangeable.

I sat there at the head of the table,
the one who'd promised to host a proper Thanksgiving,
watching my father eat candied potatoes that were just potatoes,
my sister picking at her salad because she'd become vegan
that morning, apparently, and I'd made nothing without butter,
and I thought about all those magazine photographs,
those tables laden with golden plenty and families
who look like they've never said a cruel word to each other.

But then my uncle started laughing about the flaming turkey,
and my nephew spit out his vanilla potatoes,
and someone opened the second bottle of wine,
and we were all laughing, even my grandmother,
who was laughing so hard she had to take out her teeth,
which made us laugh harder, which made the dog bark,
which made my nephew knock over the cranberry sauce—
the one thing that had turned out perfectly.

We ordered pizza around seven.
We ate it on paper plates in the living room,
and my father said it was the best Thanksgiving he could remember,
and I think he might have meant it,
or maybe he was just drunk on vanilla fumes and relief,
but I sat there on the couch, holding my slice,
looking around at these people I'd somehow failed
to feed properly, these faces I'd known my whole life,
and I realized that the disasters were just another way
of saying we were together, we were trying,
we were still showing up year after year
with our burnt offerings and our terrible timing,
and isn't that what gratitude looks like when you strip away
the centerpiece and the proper serving dishes—
just us, laughing at our own magnificent incompetence,
grateful no one called the fire department,
grateful the smoke detector batteries were dead,
grateful for pizza delivery and for each other,
especially for each other, God help us all.
