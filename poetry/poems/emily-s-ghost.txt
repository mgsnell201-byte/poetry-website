Emily's Ghost

I used to think words were safer kept inside,

tucked away like good china in the cabinet,

the kind you only bring out for special occasions

that never quite arrive. You know the ones—

spotless, unused, gathering dust in the dark

while you eat off paper plates.



But Emily Dickinson, buttoned up in Amherst,

said something strange about this.

She claimed a word is dead when it's said,

though some people think the opposite is true.

I'm with the some people, though not

for any reason Emily would have approved of.



A word inside your head is like a goldfish

in a bowl—safe, sure, but also going nowhere,

swimming the same small circle day after day.

Say it out loud and suddenly it's in the ocean,

which is to say it's in trouble, picking up

barnacles and plastic bags and someone else's meaning.



This is what we mean by living, I think—

getting passed around like a rumor at a party,

arriving at the other end of the room

completely different from how you started,

carrying fingerprints and lipstick stains

and three versions of the truth.



Emily was right that the word begins its life

the moment it leaves your mouth, but she forgot

to mention how exhausting that is, how it wanders

from ear to ear getting more confused,

until finally it collapses on a couch somewhere,

meaning everything and nothing, tired and alive.
