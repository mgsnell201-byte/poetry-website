What We Agree To

I've been thinking about those two words lately,

how they sound like the smallest possible answer

to the largest possible question,

like saying "sure" when someone asks if you believe in gravity.



I do. As if we're confirming something already true,

the way you might nod when the waitress asks

if you want coffee, except the coffee is the rest of your life

and you haven't seen the menu.



My neighbor asked me once what it felt like to say them,

standing there in front of everyone you know

and some people you don't, aunts who still call you

by your childhood nickname, your grandfather

who tells the same three stories at every gathering.



I told him it felt like signing a contract

written in a language you almost understand,

or agreeing to take a trip with someone

who has the map but you have the compass,

and neither of you is entirely sure which direction is north.



Because "I do" doesn't mean "I understand"

or "I've figured this out" or "I promise never to leave

wet towels on the bathroom floor,"

though that would be a nice bonus.



It means something more like "I'm willing to find out,"

which is less romantic than the songs suggest

but probably more honest, the way a house key

is less exciting than a love letter

but more useful when it's raining.



I think about those words now when I'm loading the dishwasher

or choosing paint colors for the hallway

or trying to remember if we're out of milk—

all these tiny agreements inside the larger one,

little "I do's" scattered through the ordinary hours

like breadcrumbs that lead you home.



The phrase itself is so plain, so unadorned,

like a wooden chair or a glass of water,

functional and humble, doing its job

without calling attention to itself,

which is maybe the point—



that the biggest promises should sound the smallest,

that forever should fit inside two syllables,

that love should be as simple as a handshake

and as complicated as everything that follows,

which is breakfast and arguments and forgiveness

and the way you learn to sleep beside another person,

their breath becoming part of the room's weather,

the landscape you wake to every morning,

familiar and surprising, known and unknowable,



like those two words we spoke once

and keep speaking, quietly, without speaking,

in every door we open, every light we turn off,

every time we come home to the same address,

the same face across the table,

the same answer to the same impossible question:

yes, still, I do.
