Our First December

I want to tell you about the argument

we had on Christmas Eve about the tree,

not a real argument, more like a negotiation

between her family's insistence on white lights

and my childhood allegiance to the colored bulbs

that made our living room look like a carnival

had crashed into a forest and decided to stay.



We stood there in the Target aisle for twenty minutes,

holding two different boxes, each of us

silently computing the cost of surrendering

this particular piece of our past,

as if the wrong choice might erase

every Christmas we'd ever known before we knew each other.



In the end, we bought both.



Now our tree looks like it can't make up its mind,

white lights on the left side fading into green and red and blue

on the right, a luminous compromise,

or maybe a metaphor for marriage itself—

this daily practice of making room

for someone else's memories inside your own.



She hangs her grandmother's glass angel

next to my ridiculous wooden Rudolph

whose nose fell off in 1987 and was glued back on crooked.

I watch her arranging the ornaments,

moving them slightly, then moving them back,

the way she's learning the shape of our shared life,

testing the weight of what stays and what can be let go.



Last night we tried to decide whose family recipe

to use for Christmas cookies, and ended up

making both batches, her mother's spritz

dusted with sugar, my aunt's Mexican wedding cakes

dissolving on the tongue like edible snow.

The kitchen smelled like both our childhoods at once,

a scent that had never existed in the world before.



This morning, our first Christmas morning as a we,

I woke to find her sitting by the tree

in my old bathrobe, drinking coffee,

looking at the presents we'd wrapped for each other

with the concentrated attention

she usually reserves for crossword puzzles or maps.



When I asked what she was thinking,

she said she was trying to memorize this moment—

the particular angle of December light

coming through our window, the sound

of the radiator clanking to life, the way the cat

had chosen to sleep on top of her present instead of mine.



She said she wanted to remember this

as the first of everything we'd build together,

the first in a long catalog of mornings

that would someday feel inevitable,

as if they'd always been ours.



And I realized that's what we're doing here—

not abandoning the Christmases we had before,

but planting them like seeds in the same soil,

waiting to see what new species might grow

from this careful grafting, this deliberate cross-pollination

of her history and mine.



The tree blinks on and off,

half in white, half in color,

neither fully one thing nor the other,

but something we made together from scratch,



which is, I suppose, exactly what we are now.
