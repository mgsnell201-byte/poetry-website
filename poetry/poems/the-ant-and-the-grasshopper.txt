The Ant and the Grasshopper

All morning I have been sitting at this desk
with a cup of coffee going cold beside me,
watching through the window as a few leaves
let go of the maple and take their time
falling, the way someone might set down
a heavy bag at the end of a long walk.

And I was thinking about the grasshopper,
the one from the fable, the improvident one
who fiddled all summer — actually fiddled,
as if Aesop had handed him a tiny violin
and a chair in the tall grass
and said, "Go ahead, see what happens."

Meanwhile the ant, his neighbor,
was making trip after trip to the storehouse,
carrying a grain of wheat on his back
like a man hauling a sofa up four flights of stairs
alone, because no one he knew
was available that particular Saturday.

You know how the story ends.
Winter arrives and the grasshopper knocks
on the ant's door, shivering, apologetic,
and the ant, who has been organizing his pantry
by color and date, turns him away
with a small, practical speech about foresight.

But here is what I keep wondering
as the November light does what it does
to the ordinary surfaces of this room —
the coffee, the desk, the pen I am holding
like some kind of stubborn instrument
I still believe will work.

What did the grasshopper play?
And was there an evening in July
when the ant paused on the road
with his impossible grain of wheat
and heard a few notes drift over the wall,
something slow in a minor key,

and stood there, just stood there,
the wheat heavy on his back,
the light beginning to leave the fields,
listening for a moment longer
than he would ever admit to anyone,
before he turned and walked on?
