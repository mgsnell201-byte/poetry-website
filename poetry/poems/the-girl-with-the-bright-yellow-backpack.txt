The Girl with the Bright Yellow Backpack

I used to see her every morning at the bus stop,

the girl with Down syndrome and the bright yellow backpack

decorated with cartoon characters I couldn't quite name,

standing next to what I assumed was her mother or grandmother,

someone patient, someone who seemed to know

the exact number of steps from the front door to the curb.



She would wave at the crossing guard,

at the man walking his terrier,

at me on the occasions when I happened to glance up

from my phone or my coffee or whatever urgent emptiness

I was attending to that particular week.

Her hand moved through the air like a small flag

or a bird that hadn't quite learned the mechanics of flight.



I suppose I waved back sometimes,

offered that casual nod we reserve for people

we see regularly but don't actually know,

the way you might acknowledge a painting

that hangs in a hallway you pass through

on your way to somewhere else.



Then one Tuesday or Wednesday—I can't remember which—

the bus stop was empty.

And the next day. And the next.

I found myself looking for that yellow backpack

the way you might look for a missing button

or a word that sits just beyond the edge of your tongue.



I never learned her name.

I never asked why she stopped appearing,

whether they moved away or changed their route

or something else I don't want to catalogue here.

But sometimes now, when I'm standing in line

or waiting for a light to change,

I think about all the small, persistent greetings

I failed to return with anything resembling attention,

how her hand kept moving through the air

like someone conducting an orchestra

only she could hear,

and how I never once thought to ask

what music she was listening to.
