The First Lilly

I came down to the pond before first light
and stood at the edge where the reeds begin.
Mist lay across the dark water
like breath on a mirror, and already
the first lily had opened—
its white petals loosening
one by one into the soft gray air,
each one catching the smallest rumor of gold.

A turtle slid from its log without sound,
leaving a single silver ring that widened
and disappeared. Frogs called low and steady,
not yet the full chorus, but the first
tentative voices testing the morning.
I watched a dragonfly hover,
its wings beating so fast they vanished,
then settle on a blade of grass still bent
with last night’s rain.

Everything was waking
in its own quiet way—
the water striders sketching their thin lines,
the small fish rising to take the light
that had begun to slant through the trees.
I tell you, there is such tenderness here,
in the slow unfurling of petals,
in the patient return of green to the banks.

And therefore I stay longer than I meant to,
letting the pond teach me again
how to be present, how to love
what is brief and shining and ordinary.
You could do worse than to come here
at this hour and simply stand,
letting the world open itself
without asking anything back.

The lily keeps lifting its face
toward whatever arrives next,
and the dark water holds it all—
reflection and root, beginning and end—
in one clear, unbroken silence.
