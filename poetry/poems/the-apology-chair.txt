The Apology Chair

There was a chair in the hallway
outside my childhood bedroom—
wooden, uneven, and perfectly designed
for saying you're sorry.
You sat on it after slamming the door
or shouting shut up at your sister,
or for that day I decided
it would be funny
to put my goldfish in the bathtub.

The rule was simple:
say you're sorry,
and mean it—though how exactly
one measures sincerity at age nine
remains a mystery
to both children and the adults
they are slowly turning into.

I remember once
trying to apologize
for knocking over a potted plant
with my plastic lightsaber,
but got distracted midway
by a loose thread in my sock,
and the tiny, sudden idea
that maybe the plant
wanted to fall.

What I'm saying is,
we’re taught so early
to perform regret
like it’s a school play—
you remember your lines,
bow at the end,
and hope no one notices
you forgot the part
where you feel it.

Still, I’ve kept a chair like that
in the back of my mind—
not for punishment
but for pause,
a place to sit
when the days get too loud,
or when I start to believe
that being sorry
is the same as being forgiven.

It isn’t, of course.
But sometimes,
when no one's around,
I sit there anyway
and tell the goldfish
I’m still sorry.
