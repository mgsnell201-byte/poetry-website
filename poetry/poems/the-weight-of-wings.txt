The Weight of Wings

They expect my pen to carve borders,
to bleed ink into manifestos.
But I long to trace the morning’s light
fracturing through my grandmother’s teacup—
golden, fragile, untouched by history’s weight.

Yet sugar dissolves into ash here,
each granule a village unmade,
each stir a protest I did not choose.

The editor’s voice crackles:
“Your words for the resistance issue.”
Outside, a bird sings—
not of freedom, not of cages,
just a thread of sound,
unraveling the sky’s silence.

My homeland nests in my syntax,
but my syntax carries me elsewhere—
a kite cut loose, drifting over alien fields.

In English, I am a stranger,
borrowing vowels like borrowed shoes,
slipping on their unfamiliar edges.
In Arabic, I am a ghost,
haunting my tongue’s eroded map,
words like stones too heavy to throw.

What is a poet without a wound?
What is a wound without its poet?
The question spins through my notebooks,
a threadless needle stitching air.

My mother tongue tastes of uprooted roots,
my adopted language fits like a stranger’s coat—
too tight, too loose,
whispering: You are not home.

Tonight, I try to write of stars,
weightless, innocent.
By dawn, they are eyes—
refugees’ gazes, border lights,
holes torn in the sky’s fragile cloth.

No poem escapes the weight of wings.
To breathe is to resist,
to write is to carry
a homeland that no longer holds you,
a verse that sings itself
into silence.
