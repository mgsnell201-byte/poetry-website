Skies Within Skies

I was sitting in a chair by the window

with a cup of coffee and a book

when I came across the line about the brain

being wider than the sky,

and I have to admit I looked up

at the actual sky just then,

which was doing its usual thing—

a few clouds drifting by like aimless shoppers,

a jet trail dissolving into nothing.



This reclusive poet says the brain can contain the sky,

hold it inside with room to spare,

and I suppose she's right,

because there I was with the whole Friday sky

tucked into my head,

clouds and all,

plus the memory of last Friday's sky,

and the sky from when I was seven,

standing in my grandmother's yard.



The thing is, this poem's without a title,

just a simple number 632

as if the idea was too large for a name,

or maybe she just forgot,

the way I forget where I put my keys

even though my brain is supposedly wider than the sky.

And yet—here's the thing—I couldn't argue with her.



Sitting here, holding her book,

I did contain her sky,

or some approximation of it. Clouds

she'd watched from Amherst in whatever year

were drifting through my skull like refugees,

making themselves at home in the same space

where I keep all my squalid little memories,

my grocery lists, my knowledge of bus routes,

the lyrics to songs I wish I could forget.



Perhaps that's what she meant: not that the mind

is grand, but that it's helplessly roomy—

a junk shop where the sublime and idiotic

share the same dusty shelf. The infinite sky

crammed in beside the argument you had

last Tuesday, and the thing you said at work

that made you cringe, and your mother's maiden name,

and the exact expression on that woman's face

when you walked past her on the stairs and couldn't

remember who she was, though clearly, clearly,

she remembered you. All of it in there,

taking up the same amount of space,

which is to say: no space at all, and yet

enough to make you wake at 3 a.m. and stare.



And now here I sit with her brain

inside my brain,

her sky inside my sky,

and I wonder if this is what she meant—

that we keep adding rooms,

one consciousness inside another,

like Russian dolls made of light and memory,

each one wider than we think,

each one holding more

than it has any business holding,

until you can't tell anymore

where the sky ends

and the thinking begins.
