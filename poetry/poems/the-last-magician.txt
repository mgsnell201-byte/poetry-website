The Last Magician

We build our houses card by card, each morning

a new floor added to the tower

that will not stand—yet still we climb

the shaking stairs of maybe,

clutch at railings made of smoke.



Death, the last magician, watches

from the wings, cape sewn

with galaxies and grave dirt,

shuffling his deck of heartbeats

while we applaud our own performance.



How we rehearse immortality! Practice

walking through walls that exist,

swallow swords of steel and promises,

pull rabbits from hats that hold

only darkness and the sound

of our mothers calling us home.



The final trick: one moment

you are bread becoming toast,

steam rising like a small prayer—

the next, you are absence

wearing your favorite shirt.



And oh, the beautiful wreckage

you leave scattered: half-finished

crossword puzzles bleeding ink,

books with broken spines

still whispering their stories

to empty rooms.



Your children inherit this museum

of interrupted gestures—

the way you stirred your coffee

counterclockwise, the exact angle

of your reading glasses

abandoned on the nightstand

like two moons that forgot

how to rise.



They learn the cruelest alchemy:

how love transmutes to memory,

how your laughter becomes archaeology

they excavate from old voicemails,

how grief is just love

with nowhere left to go,

so it pools in their chests

like water in a waiting cup.



Death deals the same hand

to every player, yet still we bet

our small eternities on tomorrow,

still we shuffle through our days

as if the house always wins

for everyone but us.
