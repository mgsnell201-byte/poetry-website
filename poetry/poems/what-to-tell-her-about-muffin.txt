What to Tell Her About Muffin

She's standing in the doorway asking
where's Muffin, her jacket still on,
backpack dragging on the floor, and I'm thinking
how do I explain this without lying,
without making death sound like a trip
he's taking, some farm upstate where dogs
run free and no one ever comes back?

I've been rehearsing all week—gentle
versions, clinical versions, the truth
dressed up in soft language like a body
in a casket wearing someone else's clothes.
But she's six and waiting and I can feel
the weight of what I have to say
pressing against my ribs like grief,

like the way Muffin used to lean
his whole body into my legs
when he wanted something, anything,
just to be close. She loved that dog
the way kids love—completely, without
the knowledge that what you love
can stop breathing while you sleep.

So I tell her: Muffin's heart got old.
His body stopped working. He's gone.
And I watch her face do that thing
faces do when they're learning
the world can take things back,
that love doesn't guarantee
keeping, that nothing does.

She asks if it hurt and I lie—
I say no, though I don't know,
though I held him while the vet
found the vein, felt him go slack
and heavy in my arms like a coat
falling off a hanger. What else
can you tell a child? The truth

is a luxury we can't afford yet.
She's crying now, asking why,
and I want to say: because bodies
fail, because time is a bastard,
because every good thing you love
will leave you or you'll leave it
and that's just how it goes—

but instead I pull her close,
let her sob into my shirt,
her small hands gripping my arms
like I'm the only solid thing left
in a world that just learned
to shift and crack beneath her.
Later, she asks if we can get another dog

and I almost laugh at how quickly
she moves through it, this first lesson
in loss, while I'm still standing here
holding his empty bowl, unable
to throw it away, unable to fill it,
caught between what was
and what will never be again.

Outside, the street is loud with living—
cars, voices, someone's music
bleeding through an open window.
Inside, we're learning the same
old human trick: how to stand upright
when the ground keeps opening,
how to breathe through the breaking.
