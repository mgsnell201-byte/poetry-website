The Right Amount

At the desk this morning, next to the lamp
still burning from last night's reading,
I come across the old definition again—
black as absence, the color that is not a color,
the empty theater before the play begins.

But then I think of the photographs
pinned above my daughter's bed:
the Pillars of Creation, the Ring Nebula,
each one mostly black, mostly nothing,
and still we cannot stop looking.

Black is what holds the stars apart,
what gives them room to burn,
the way silence gives shape to music,
or the margin frames the page
where someone has underlined a single word.

It occurs to me that we have gotten this backwards.
What if black is not the absence of color
but the presence of all of them at once,
perfectly balanced, each one accounted for—
red and blue and yellow and green
mixed so carefully, so completely,
that they arrive at a kind of peace,
the way voices in a choir
become a single note?

I remember standing in a museum once,
watching a painter demonstrate
how every color on his palette,
when mixed together long enough,
became a shade of black or gray—
not emptiness, he said,
but fullness taken to its logical end,
all arguments resolved,
all claims settled.

Maybe this is why we lean toward black
when we want other colors to speak:
the red dress against the evening,
the white sail on dark water,
the yellow leaves of October
against the wet bark of the oak.

Black does not erase them.
It gives them a place to stand,
the way a stage goes dark
so we can see the single candle,
the way the sky dims at dusk
so we can finally notice
how green the grass has been all day.

I have stood on a beach in the islands
where the sky and water were both so dark
they became the same black fabric,
seamless, salt-edged,
and the only light was phosphorescence
breaking where my hands moved through the waves—
blue-green fire, cold and brief,
made visible only by the blackness
that held it, that gave it somewhere to burn.

Even the canvas, before the painter arrives,
wants to be white, we think,
but how many paintings begin in darkness,
the artist building light on top of shadow,
laying down black first
so the colors that come after
have something to push against,
some weight to measure themselves by?

Maybe black is not too little
but exactly enough—
not the absence of red or blue or gold
but their sum, their total,
the place where they all agree to rest.

The right amount of color
for the moon to float in,
for the ocean to remember at midnight,
for your face in the window
when you pause between sentences,
thinking of what to say next.

Even now, the black text on this white page—
these words would mean nothing
without the space around them,
and the space would mean nothing
without the words.
Each one needs the other
to make sense of what they're doing here.

So perhaps black is the conversation,
not the silence.
The fullness, not the void.
The room where all the colors went
and decided to stay,
close enough to touch,
quiet enough to hear
each other breathing.
