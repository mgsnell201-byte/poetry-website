My Grandmother Knots the Apron Twice

My grandmother knots the apron strings behind her back. The cotton snags on the dry skin of her thumbs. I press my palm into the dough, and the mound springs back, filling the hollow where my hand pressed. The yeast smells like the inside of a coat closet in March, warm and sharp. Flour drifts onto the floorboards, dusting my feet white. My brother snores through the sliver of the bedroom door, or maybe he listens, his ear tilted toward the stairwell. Grandmother folds the dough, pushes it, folds it again, and the apron pockets sway with the weight of a wooden spoon and a peppermint wrapper from last Tuesday. Last winter she wrung out a washcloth here while my mother wept behind the door she clicked shut. Now the door upstairs leans open an inch, and hallway light spills down the stairs in a thin bar across the kitchen table. The dough rises, the oven ticks, and Grandmother wipes her forehead, leaving flour across her eyebrow as she winks, her hand still buried in the soft center of the bread we will eat at dinner. I find the drawer pull with my shoulder blade, and the door exhales, the apron strings tighten, the dough keeps the print of every hand that shaped it. The yeast bubbles in the bowl. Flour drifts through the hallway sliver and settles on the stairs.
