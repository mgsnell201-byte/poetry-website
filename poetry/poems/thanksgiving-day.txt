Thanksgiving Day

I invite you in—take off your shoes, sit
at this kitchen table where I peel potatoes
and try to count my gratitude like these lumps of skin—
unwanted, earthy, each with its salt and slip.

I want to thank you for the dawn that yawns open the curtains,
for the coffee’s bitter first sip, for the spoon balanced
between trembling and relief.
Thank you for the neighbor’s car alarm at three
reminding me I’m alive, even when sleep has left the room.

Thank you for the sticky sheet of honey on the counter,
for bread that breaks in my hands, warm and flawed—
those crumbs are confessions, familiar in their mess.
I’m grateful for that cat’s arched back by the radiator, the way steam
bends its hair into flames.

Thank you, too, for mistakes—burned pies, misread maps,
for saying “I love you” when I mean “I’m sorry,” or vice versa,
for compassion’s awkward stumble. Behold the tapestry woven
from apologies and laughter, from small failures held together
by stubborn threads of belonging.

I offer you thanks as if laying a table: imperfect, sincere—
such gratitude cannot be grandiose. It is the feather in the teacup,
the crack in the porch step where grass insists on living.

Because thank you isn’t about having everything right—
it’s about recognizing that I’ve been given breath, sky,
wide open hands and sometimes trembling ones—
and still I choose to give back this: my thanks.
