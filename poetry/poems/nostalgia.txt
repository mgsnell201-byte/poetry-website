Nostalgia

Sixty-eight years, but I am still
that eight-year-old, standing before
the stacked logs where my dog Chipper disappeared,
chasing the brown flicker of squirrel
into darkness between oak and ash.

I called his name the way a child calls—
believing voice alone could pull him back,
that love was loud enough to move through wood.
But only silence answered,
and I knew with sudden certainty:
the squirrel had eaten him.

This was how the world worked then—
things vanished absolutely,
and I could save them if I tried hard enough.
So I began dismantling the pile,
log by log, my hands learning
the weight of each piece,
my voice growing hoarse,
tears starting their own logic.

When the last log lay aside
and no Chipper emerged,
I sat down in the clearing I'd created,
sobbing into the space where he should have been.

The walk back taught me how grief moves—
a few steps forward, then turning
to scan the empty field behind me.
Forward. Turn. Call his name.
Again. Again.

The tears made the path strange.
I took a wrong turn, suddenly lost
in the landscape I'd known all morning.
Only my grandmother's white house,
distant as a star, pulled me forward—
head down, voice breaking on his name.

The gate wavered through my blurred vision.
I was still calling, still turning back
to search the field's expanse,
when he exploded from the bushes—
tail wild, fur warm under my hands,
whole, impossibly alive.

I cried then, too.
Different tears that meant:
you came back,
the world gave you back,
I don't have to carry
this breaking anymore.

What remains is not the mountains
I imagined in those hills,
not the vastness I invented,
but this: the specific weight of logs,
the exact distance between loss and gate,
the way Chipper's warmth felt
under my eight-year-old fingers,
still learning what it means
when something beloved
disappears,
and then—unasked for, unbargained—
returns.
