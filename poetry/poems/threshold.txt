Threshold

I have been thinking about the word joy

and how strange it is that we gave it to daughters—

actual daughters, named it right onto them,

as if a child could hold

what the rest of us keep dropping.



Three letters. One syllable.

You can say it while barely opening your mouth,

which seems like a design flaw

given everything it is expected to carry.



I looked it up, as one does when a word

starts behaving suspiciously.

Old French. Latin before that.

And before that, probably a woman

in a bright room, already crying,

which is where I want to begin.



She is not crying from joy.

Let us not rush past that

toward the prettier explanation.

She is crying from pain—

the kind that lives in the back, the hips,

the place below the ribs

that has no polite name

because no one wants to linger there.



And then a cry answers hers.

Small. New. Furious with life.



And something arrives inside the pain

the way light does not erase shadows

but quietly changes what they mean.



And she weeps. Still from pain.

Now also from the other thing.

The body, that faithful and literal instrument,

cannot tell you which is which.

It is using the same word for both.

The word is not a word.

It is water.



She names him morning.

She names him river.

She does not name him temporary,

though that would be the accurate name,

the one that holds up under scrutiny,

the one no parent has ever had the heart to use.



Every name is a small forgetting,

a door set into the wall of loss

and painted a cheerful color

so you do not notice, for a while,

what it is set into.



Her words bloom around him at the breakfast table—

rose-gold above the cereal, above the small face

that has already stopped hearing I love you

as anything but furniture.

She says it again.

Which is its own kind of question.

Which is, underneath everything,

a quiet pain—

the pain of loving someone

who is already, imperceptibly,

growing away.



Not gone.

But leaving in the slow way

that is almost worse,

the way that was always the point,

which does not make it

not hurt.



Meanwhile, in another room—

less light, different year, same woman—

grief has moved in without asking.

It lives in the chest, a stone

that crossed from outside to inside

sometime in the night.

It lives in the stomach.

In the throat.

In the sound that gets stuck

behind something that will not move.



Grief, it turns out, is pain.

Different occasion. Same body.

Same nerve sending the same message

it always sends when something

exceeds the size of silence.



The salt runs down the same face,

the same rivers,

the same chin.

Falls.



Those who did not know her then

say: grief.

Those who knew her in the bright room

say nothing—

because they remember the other falling water

and they cannot tell you,

standing here,

if these are different tears

or the same tear,

grown older,

finally arrived.



This is the thing about pain:

it is not the third emotion.

It is the ground.

Joy stands on it.

Grief stands on it.

Every large and unsayable thing

stands on it

and looks out at the view

and does not mention the floor.



So when you see someone crying

and reach for the nearest word—

joy, you think, or grief, depending

on the occasion and your own recent losses—

know that you are probably wrong.

Or right about the occasion

and wrong about the ground.



Tears are not a window.

They are a signal:

something here has crossed a line.

The body marks all three the same way.

It was only the words

that kept insisting on the difference,

that kept building borders

on a floor that was always

one floor.



The mother who wept when he arrived

and the mother who wept when he was gone

is the same mother.



She has never left the doorway.



Not because she cannot move—

she has moved, she has made coffee,

she has said I love you above the cereal

to children who have stopped listening—

but because threshold is not a line you cross.

It is the place you become.

The place all three arrive

and do not leave,

and you learn, slowly,

to call it home.



She does not choose a word.

She lets the water speak.

She lets the salt

do its ancient work,

which it has been doing

long before we had names for any of this,

and will be doing

long after the names

are gone.
