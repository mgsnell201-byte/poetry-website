The Vanishing Point

You're asking the wrong question, friend,

sitting there with your poems spread across the kitchen table

like tarot cards you can't quite read.

You want to know if the work is good

before you've finished making it good,

which is like asking if the bread will rise

while your hands are still kneading the dough.



I'll tell you what I think, though I'm just a guy

who writes poems in the morning and walks his dog in the afternoon,

which might make me the least qualified person

or the most, depending on your mood.



The difference between da Vinci and Van Gogh

isn't that one got roses at the curtain call

and the other got a bullet and a wheat field.

It's that both of them kept working

when the work was all they had,

when the canvas was the only mirror that didn't lie.



You say if you alone read it, it's vanity.

But I bet that you bend over a notebook

the way a jeweler examines a stone,

adjusting each facet, testing how the light

moves through the words. That's not vanity.

That's faith—the kind that doesn't require

an audience or a panel of judges in robes.



Sure, we send our poems out into the world

hoping someone will catch them like fireflies in a jar,

but the poem was complete the moment you finished it,

the way a tree falling in the forest makes a sound

whether or not the philosophers are listening.



The value isn't independent of you—it's just

independent of their recognition,

which is a different animal entirely.

You'll write a thousand poems that nobody reads

and they'll still be poems,

the same way a bird sings in the morning

whether or not you're awake to hear it.



So keep your hands in the dough, my friend.

The bread will rise or it won't,

but the rising was never the point.

The point was that you needed to make something

that didn't exist before you made it,

and that need—that quiet, persistent need—

is the only validation that ever mattered.
