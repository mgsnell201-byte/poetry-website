My Friend in Rhyme

October brought him, tapping at my door,
A stack of books, a wooden raven he bore,
He set it on my shelf, and there it's perched since then,
"For luck," he said, "or maybe just for when."

He reads my drafts with weary, sunken eyes,
He circles every rhyme too worn, too wise,
"Your meter falters here — it's flat, it's sore,"
Then sighs and shakes his head, and frowns once more.

He caught me Googling once, some app to compose,
He blanched a shade whiter — something in him froze,
"A machine writes your heart? That's the coldest of jokes,
I've outdueled worse rivals than silicon folks."

I asked for his birth year — he coughed and turned pale,
Said "eighteen-oh-nine," then went thin as a veil,
I reached out to touch him — my hand passed right through,
"Ah," he said, "right. I forgot to tell you."

Sometimes he speaks of someone, a girl named Lenore,
Lost long ago to fever, back in Baltimore,
His voice drops to a hush like a bell tolling slow,
And something in the floorboards seems to beat, soft and low.

So he's been dead two centuries, give or take a few,
Still critiquing my meter as dead poets do,
A raven, a Lenore, a heartbeat, a door —
He'll haunt my bad couplets forever and more.
