Passing notice

I drove the old truck down the river road
where morning light lay thin across the fields,
and felt my mother’s care still in the wheel—
how she had handed me the keys that day,
her hand steady as the years had made it.

In the glove compartment, folded once,
I found my father’s obituary,
the paper worn along its creases now,
ink faded where her fingers must have touched
it many times in secret, keeping watch.

The pasture opened on my left, the grass
still wet with dew, and the apple tree
stood heavy with its fruit, remembering
the hands that planted it and pruned its limbs
through all the seasons of their quiet life.

Yet loss comes to us as the rain comes,
slow and certain, turning the good earth dark.
I thought how they had kept their vows to land
and to each other, working side by side
through frost and drought, through joy and wearing down.

We learn too late the weight of what is given—
a mother’s final carefulness, a father’s
name set down in black and white, the years
they held between them like a well-kept field.
The obligation stays, to tend what they began.

I stopped the truck beside the fenceline, cut
the engine, and let the silence settle in.
Two hawks turned high above the ridge, riding
the same wind that once moved across their faces.
The work remains. The love remains. I go on.
