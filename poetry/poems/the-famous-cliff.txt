The Famous Cliff

So you've driven all this way, what, three hours?

Four? The GPS kept losing signal

in the mountains and you had to guess

which turnoff, which dirt road rutted

with last winter's damage. You're here now.



The famous cliff. The one everyone talks about

like it's some kind of pilgrimage site,

some destination for the truly done.



And it's beautiful, you have to admit.



The ocean down there working its shift,

all that indifferent crashing and foam,

salt stinging your lips like regret,

the rocks like broken teeth, the gulls

wheeling and screaming their gull screams—

let's be honest—like rusty hinges,

but nothing like transcendence.



You expected a sign. A guardrail.

Something official saying "Don't," or "Do Not,"

or "Here Lies the Body of Common Sense."

But there's just dirt and scrub grass and the edge,

that clean line where the earth decides

it's had enough of being solid.



You could do it. That's the thing.

You could actually do it. No one's here

to stop you, no one's going to rush over

with a blanket and a hotline number.

It's just you and the physics of it,

gravity's old math.



But here's what's weird: you're hungry.

Your stomach is actually growling,

which seems like a betrayal, like your body

hasn't gotten the memo about despair.

And you're thinking about that taco truck

you passed twenty miles back,

its hand‑painted sign flickering under bulbs,

steam rising from the grill like a promise,

and whether they're still open,

and whether the al pastor is any good.



You're thinking: I drove all this way.

You're thinking: I should at least see it through.

But you're also thinking:

Tacos.

I need to pee.

My phone's almost dead.



What if I just stood here a while,

what if I just looked at it, this edge,

this drop into nothing,

and then turned around?



Would that make me a coward or a survivor?

Would that make me someone who chose

the taco truck over the abyss,

the mundane over the mythic,

the stupid animal's need to eat

over the grand gesture?



The wind picks up. It's cold.

It whips your hair, chills your bones—whispers:

Go back.

You're wearing the wrong jacket.

You didn't plan for this—the weather of your ending:

your nose running, your hands shaking,

your teeth chattering,

not from fear but from being human,

cold, alive.



You take a step back. Then another.

The cliff doesn't care. The ocean

keeps erasing itself.



And you—you're already thinking

about the drive back, about whether

you can find that taco truck in the dark,

about how you'll tell this story later,

or maybe you won't tell it at all.



Maybe you'll just keep it, this moment

when you stood at the rim of the world

and chose, for no good reason, to stay.



The GPS beeps low battery as you turn,

the gulls creak overhead.



I stayed. The ocean kept erasing itself.

That was my choice.
