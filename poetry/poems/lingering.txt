Lingering

Here is what no one tells you: the rule that bends is not the one you'd choose. Sound stays. Every syllable hangs in the air like slow smoke — visible for days, for weeks, for as long as it carried something true, which is to say some words never leave at all.

Walk any city. The streets are thick with it. Lovers' whispers braided pale between buildings. Arguments gone to wire, snagging sleeves, throats, the soft place under the jaw. A mother's I love you at the breakfast table loops gold above the milk jug, above the cereal, above the child who has already stopped hearing it — fading only when she says it again, which is its own kind of question.

And the boy in the school hallway, his I'm fine drifting ash-gray, neither rising nor settling, waiting — for what, exactly? Someone to look. Someone to not look away. The ash goes on drifting. This is not a metaphor. This is the physics of the thing.

This morning I told my daughter her pancakes were perfect. I watched the words bloom and twine, rose-gold, around her small wrists like bracelets, like a gentle binding, and she smiled, and I could not tell — still cannot — whether what I made was a gift or a debt she will spend years learning to name.

At night the city layers: old lullabies beneath first confessions beneath last words, an archaeology no one commissioned and no one can stop. Some corners are unbreathable with it. Others shine. You learn to move through both. You learn, which is not the same as you understand, which is not the same as it stops.
