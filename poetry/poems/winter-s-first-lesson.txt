Winter's First Lesson

I want you to picture this:

a single pane of kitchen glass

starting to cloud in the lower right corner,

as if the cold is learning to write its name

in a language of dew.



The coffee maker sighs its final drip,

and I notice the cat curling tighter on the rug,

a furry comma between morning and what comes next.



Outside, the pine tree shivers—

not from wind, but from the thought of ice,

the way a student might flinch before a pop quiz

on seasonal etiquette.



You’d think after eons of practice,

winter would remember its entrance,

but here it is, fumbling with the doorknob,

leaving damp fingerprints on the handle.



I pull a sweater over my head,

the wool smelling faintly of cedar,

and realize I’m wearing preparation,

not yet necessity—

a costume for a play that hasn’t quite begun.



The radio plays a song I’ve never heard,

the lyrics about trains and stars

and other things that keep moving

while the world holds its breath.



And I smile because the absurdity is perfect:

a planet spinning through space,

and winter still learning to tie its shoes,

one frozen lace at a time.



Later, I’ll write a note to myself

on the fogged-up mirror:

Notice how the ordinary conspires

to make the strange feel like home.



But for now, I just watch the window’s edge

whiten like the margins of an unwritten page,

knowing the story will come,

but not yet—

not quite yet.
