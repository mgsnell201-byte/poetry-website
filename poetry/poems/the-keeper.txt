The Keeper

They told me, "Put away your childish things,"

So I packed the wonder up in reasoned, tidy boxes.

I learned the weight of words, the chill that silence brings,

And walked through days bordered by clockses.



But a stubborn part, a seed they could not find,

Refused to wither in the dark and settled dust.

It held the scent of rain on hot concrete, the kind

That smells of promise, of a world reborn and just.



It kept the memory of the snail's engraved trail,

The dizzy spin of a maple's helicopter seed,

The patient art of building a leaf-boat to sail

In the river of a gutter—a singular, mighty deed.



So now I walk with two pairs of eyes.

One sees the path, the storm, the falling night.

The other watches where the lightborn insect flies

And turns the mundane world to sudden, stunning light.



I sign the forms and pay the bills on time,

My hands are scored with lines of work and care,

Yet I still taste the sun in a clementine,

And find a universe trapped in a ladybug's lair.



The grief I've known is a deep and weathered stone,

It sits inside me, permanent and cold.

But it makes the joy, when a simple joy is known,

A sharper, brighter fire to have and hold.



For the child does not forget, the man does not regret,

The trade is off; the two are woven tight.

The years give depth to what the young heart sees,

And the young heart sets the older world alight.



I am not old, not young, but something in between—

A keeper of the key, who never lost the door.

The world is serious, brittle, sharp, and keen,

And I love it all so fiercely, now, much more than before.
