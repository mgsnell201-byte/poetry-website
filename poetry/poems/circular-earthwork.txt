Circular Earthwork

The word rath — as in the fort

on the drumlin above the Moy —

is not the same word as wrath,



though the mouth that says one

can't always say which. Ráth

means rampart, circular earthwork,



and has no part in the wrath of God

that scoured the Egyptians,

though the drumlin holds its own



prescriptions: sphagnum, sedge,

the peat that seals what it receives.

Below the feet of the horse



that grazed there — a roan gelding,

Mullan's, fetlock-scarred —

the ground was keeping something.



Whether body, bronze, or nothing,

nothing the peat disclosed.

You cut and cut and find



the face of what was cut before.

The mind works like a bog,

a colleague once remarked



at a hotel outside Armagh

built on a former marsh —

meaning it preserves, meaning



its dá, its two, its doubleness,

is what it does: the thing remembered

and the thing that was the thing



before the memory revised it.

Rath and wrath: unrelated,

philologists confirm. One predated



the other by some centuries.

Neither borrowed from the other.

Neither owes the other anything.



The roan is gone. The fort is gone.

The word alone stands, earthed,

holding what it holds,



which can't be unearthed.
