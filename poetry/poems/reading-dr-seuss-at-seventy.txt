Reading Dr. Seuss at Seventy

I found myself this morning reading "The Sneetches"
to no one in particular, just the coffee maker
and the morning light slanting through the kitchen window,
and I got to thinking about the machinery of it all—

that contraption Sylvester McMonkey McBean rolls into town,
the one that adds stars to bellies for three dollars,
then removes them for ten.

It's the kind of machine that couldn't exist
in our world of physics and biology,
but exists perfectly well in the world
where we actually live, the one where we line up
to have our stars added or subtracted,
where we pay good money to be told
we're finally on the right side of things.

You probably remember reading this as a child,
the way I do, and thinking it was about
some distant problem, some silly creatures
on some far-off beaches, not about you
sitting there in your pajamas with your juice box,
already learning the rules.

But here's what gets me now, reading it
while the coffee brews and the refrigerator hums—
the Star-Belly Sneetches and the Plain-Belly Sneetches
eventually can't tell each other apart.
They've been through the machine so many times,
adding and removing, removing and adding,
that no one knows who started with stars
and who started without.

And that's when McBean packs up and leaves,
rolling his contraption off to the next beach,
because there's no profit in creatures
who've finally figured out they're all the same.

The coffee maker beeps.
The sun has moved higher in the window.
And I'm standing here thinking about
all the machines I've walked through,
voluntary and otherwise,
all the stars I've had added and taken away,
and wondering if, like the Sneetches,
I've been through enough times now
to forget which version of myself
I started out as,

which might be exactly the point
of reading Dr. Seuss at seventy,
in your kitchen, alone, realizing
that the books we thought were teaching children
were actually teaching the adults we'd become,
if we were paying attention.
