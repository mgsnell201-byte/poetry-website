After Fifty Winters

We meet where the sidewalk ends,

where the old oak still stretches its arms

like a tired guardian between our past and now.



Your laugh—unchanged—

a wind chime in a silent house,

startling the dust from my memory.



I count the years in the lines

around your eyes, faint as pencil marks

on a well-loved map.



We were lightning once,

all split skies and reckless thunder,

but time has made us gentle—

two rivers that braided and parted,

now finding the same sea.



Your voice still fits the shape

of my listening,

like a key turning in a lock

I thought had rusted shut.



The evening stretches between us,

thin as the scar on your wrist

from that summer we swore

we’d never grow old.



And maybe we didn’t.

Maybe we’re still those wild flames,

just banked now,

embers beneath the ash.



When we hug, I smell the ghost

of your old perfume—

like lilacs and unfinished homework—

and for a moment, the years

are just leaves

brushing against the window,

waiting to be let in.
