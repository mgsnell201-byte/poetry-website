Magical Realism for Beginners

The butterflies arrived the year García Márquez
won the Nobel Prize, which was 1982,
and they have not entirely left,
which is the sort of thing that happens
in that part of the world
and, increasingly, in my kitchen.

I want to tell you about the rain first—
how it smells before it arrives,
that particular contract between earth and sky,
and how the birds go quiet
the way people do in a room
when someone says something true.

There are flowers involved, naturally.
There are always flowers in these matters,
and water moving somewhere just offstage,
and a woman who knows more than she's saying,
which is to say, every woman
in every story worth telling.

I have been trying to write this poem
the way you press a butterfly between pages—
carefully, with the understanding
that you are preserving something
by also, slightly, destroying it.

The rain arrives.
The birds resume their commentary.
A blue morpho lands on my coffee cup
and sits there with the confidence
of something that has never once
doubted its right to be exactly
this blue, in exactly this light,

and I think: yes.
That is the borrowed miracle.
That is what Macondo smells like
in the morning,
before the ice melts,
before anyone remembers
they are supposed to be ordinary.
