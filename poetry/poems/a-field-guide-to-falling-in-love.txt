A Field Guide to Falling in Love

It is not something you can do in traffic
or while parallel parking,
though I have seen people try.

You need a table, preferably one
that has hosted a few arguments
and knows how to keep a secret.

Sit down. This sounds obvious
but most of us prefer to remain standing,
poised like sprinters at the starting line.

Clear a space between the salt shaker
and yesterday's mail—
not much, just enough for one small thing
to step forward and volunteer.

Today it might be a coffee mug
with a crack running down the handle
like a river on a crude map.

Notice how it holds liquid anyway,
how it refuses to quit being useful
despite its injury.

This is what I mean by paying attention—
not the grand orchestral sweep
but the stubborn mug that won't retire.

Now think of her standing here last Tuesday
holding this exact cup,
telling you about the car trouble
or the dream with the impossible staircase.

You weren't really listening.
You were watching the way
she wrapped both hands around the warmth,
the way some people hold onto railings.

This is step one, though calling it a step
implies you have a choice,
as if you could turn back now
and find the stairs still there behind you.

Step two: admit that you've been leaving things—
a book, a sweater, a comment
about the weather that lasts too long—
the way birds drop twigs, building something
neither of you has agreed to name.

By step three you'll notice
your body has developed opinions
about which direction is north.
North being wherever she happens to be standing.

This is not magic. This is just
the inner ear recalibrating,
the heart doing what hearts do
when they encounter evidence of another heart.

Someone should have warned you
that love is mainly a series of observations—
the way he reads cereal boxes,
the angle of her handwriting,
how they apologize to furniture when they bump into it.

You collect these details
the way some people collect stamps,
mounting them carefully in the album of your attention.

Step four might be the hardest:
let them see you without your clever lines prepared,
without the armor of your best stories.
Just you, standing in the kitchen at midnight,
eating cereal from the box like a confession.

If they stay, if they grab a handful too
and lean against the counter
as if they've been doing this for years,
you're in trouble.

The good kind of trouble,
the kind that requires rearranging
your entire idea of Tuesday.

I could tell you about step five—
the negotiations with fear,
the small treaty signed between
your desire to run and your inability to move—

but you're probably already there,
standing in your kitchen,
holding the mug with the crack,
thinking about texting something
you'll revise four times before sending.

Here's what I know:
falling in love is less about the falling
and more about the moment you realize
you've been walking on air for three weeks
and forgot to panic.

It's deciding that the crack in the cup
isn't a flaw but a history,
and history is just another word
for "everything that led to now."

So make the coffee.
Send the text.
Let the mug hold what it's meant to hold,
despite the fracture, because of it.

This is the final step,
the one that repeats every morning:
choosing to believe
that broken things can still carry love
from one person's hands to another's,
still warm, still enough.
