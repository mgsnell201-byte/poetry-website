The Rose Bed

She said, "I want no common rest,

no linen white and plain.

Go cut the roses from the east

and those wet with the rain."



He filled the room with what she asked:

red, damask, climbing, wild.

The thorns he did not think to strip.

She lay down like a child.



"And is it soft?" he said to her.

"Soft as a promise kept."

The petals gave beneath her skin.

She turned. The bedding wept.



By noon the white had turned to pink.

The red had turned to black.

She breathed the sweetness in the rot.

She did not once look back.



"Why do you stay?" he asked at last.

"The thorns have marked you through."

"Because," she said, "a softer bed

would not have felt like you."



He watched the petals fall apart,

the stems give up their hold.

She closed her eyes. The scent grew thick.

The sheets grew wet and cold.



They found her smiling in the dawn,

the wounds still on her breast.

She'd asked him for a bed of roses.

He'd given her the rest.



And what is love, the coroner wrote,

but lying down in bloom,

and learning that the sweetest things

will turn to rot and ruin?



Yet still we cut them, still we lie

in what we know will fade—

the softness and the thorn at once.

This is how love is made.
