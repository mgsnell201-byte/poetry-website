The Rite

There is a garden I return to when the light is still new,

when the mist holds the morning in pewter — silver and dew,

and one red poppy stands open alone at the edge of the bed

like a word on the tip of the tongue — not yet spoken, not dead.



The bee arrives first, as he always arrives, without haste,

a thing of pure purpose, his body a hunger, a taste

for the covenant older than Latin — he enters, she bends

the faintest degree, and he reads her, and drinks, and ascends.



The garden goes quiet the way a room empties of sound

when something essential departs. I stand fixed to the ground

and feel what I always feel here — that the giving and taking

is not what it seems, that the flower is not really breaking



but completing herself in the loss. Then he comes —

the hummingbird, green fire suspended, his wings become drums

of pure stillness — and enters, and drinks, and the poppy receives

him the way a door opens to wind, the way autumn receives



the first cold. He is gone in a heartbeat. The sun

has burned through the mist now, the long morning nearly undone

into noon, and I think it is over — but no, she arrives:

the butterfly drifts like a memory nobody contrives



to remember, just finds — Painted Lady, wings folded and bright,

who lights on the flower the way old love returns in the night,

not as hunger, but knowing. She opens. She gives. And the red

of the poppy burns deeper, as though it is fed by what's fed



from it. I close my notebook. The garden is done with its rite.

Three visitors, one small flower, one long slow morning of light —

and I, the fool standing witness with dirt on his knees,

learning again what the poppy has always known with such ease:



that to open completely is not to be emptied, but found.
