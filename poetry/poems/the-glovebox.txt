The Glovebox

The glovebox needs a good shake before it opens,
and when it does, the dog in the back seat
lifts his chin from his paws to study the situation,
as if any latching compartment in a parked car
might contain something necessary to the canine future.

Inside is the archaeology of my mother’s afternoons:
napkins from restaurants that have since closed,
a tire pressure gauge, a map folded the wrong way,
and a blue envelope with my name written in the kind of cursive
they stopped teaching two decades ago,
the letters already relaxing into the paper
like people settling onto a couch they recognize.

I was only looking for proof of insurance,
something official to wave at the authority of traffic,
but here is a letter postmarked twenty-five years ago,
its single sentence breathing with the patience
of a thing that knows everything about waiting.

This is how they taught us to approach a poem—
with clean hands and a scalpel ready,
pinning the butterfly of meaning to a corkboard
and labeling its parts until it forgot how to fly.

But then this piece of paper refuses to be tortured.
It is only a woman I used to live inside of,
sitting at a kitchen table in another century,
pressing a hospital gift-shop ballpoint into cheap stationery
to write welcome to this world son
before she knew what kind of world this was,
or whether I would turn out to be worth the welcome.

And yet the sentence asks nothing of me.
It simply waited in the dark behind the map
while the straws collected their thin white shadows,
as patient as anything that knows
it has all the time in the world.

I am sliding it back behind the napkins,
the gauge, the small history of a woman
who drove and stopped and drove again,
when the dog, who has been watching all along,
lets out a sigh that steams the cold window,
and the afternoon sunlight fills the empty passenger seat.
