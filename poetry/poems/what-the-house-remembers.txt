What the House Remembers

I have noticed, these past few weeks,
that the kitchen smells of wet ferns in the morning,
that particular green-dark smell of something
pressed and bruised underfoot,
though there are no ferns,
and the vents, when I hold my hand to them,
push out only the ordinary air
of a house doing its best.

And the counter—
I wipe it before bed, which is a kind of prayer,
and by morning there is a fine gray silt again,
distributed evenly, as if the night
had been busy with small administrative tasks.

Somewhere under the basement,
something shifts—
not a crack, not a leak,
but the low, patient complaint
of something that has been lying still
for a very long time
and is only now remembering
it had somewhere to be.

The shadows of oak trees move along the east wall
in the way shadows do when trees are swaying,
though I know, without looking,
that there are no oaks.
I have lived here seven years.
I know every tree.

The front door has been sticking lately.
I pull it toward me the way you coax
a reluctant conversation,
and it comes, eventually,
but not happily.

Last Thursday, in the bathroom mirror,
I watched myself stand in the middle of a thicket—
dark cedar and blackthorn pressing in from all sides,
the room behind me gone entirely—
while I held my toothbrush
and did not move.

I have read that forests have long memories.
That the soil keeps a record the way libraries do:
everything that stood here, everything that leaned,
every root that stitched itself through clay,
every creek that found the path of least resistance
and followed it for ten thousand years
until someone laid a foundation across its throat.

I used to think the house was haunted.
Now I think the house is borrowed.

And tonight, when the floor gives a long, slow exhale
beneath the kitchen table,
I do not check for leaks.

I simply sit still
and let the creek
take its time
finding me.
