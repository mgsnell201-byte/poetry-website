Today My Mind Declared It Nostalgia Day

This morning, I remembered how my daughter told me
the moon follows our car because it wants to play,
and I didn’t correct her.

I could have mentioned orbital mechanics,
the illusion of parallax, the way distant objects
seem to track our movement—
but why would I bring gravity to a child
who knows the moon gets lonely?

She also thought our dog spoke English
but kept quiet, saving his words
for when he really needed them,
and that clouds were practice drawings
God hadn’t erased yet.

I was thirty-five then, thinking about tax brackets,
the structural integrity of the deck,
whether that noise in the engine was serious,
whether I’d updated my password.
My daughter wondered if the moon
might like to come in for cookies.

I remember the day she asked why I looked sad
and I said I wasn’t, just tired,
which was a lie, and she knew it.
She said,
“Your face is doing the sad thing.”

And she was right.

Children are terrible at metaphor
but perfect at truth.

They haven’t learned yet that we’re supposed
to call loneliness “busy” or despair “fine,”
or to pretend the moon is only a rock
that doesn’t care about us.

Tonight when I go to bed and look back
across seventy-six years,
I’ll remember tucking her in—the small nightlight
casting a moon on the wall—how she asked,
“Dad, can the moon see through our roof?”
and I said, “Yes, absolutely,
it’s watching over you right now,”
and for once I wasn’t the one
turning something simple into a lie.
