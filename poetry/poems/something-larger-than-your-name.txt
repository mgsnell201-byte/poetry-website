Something Larger Than Your Name

I kneel at the tree line where light comes apart

in the maple leaves, each one holding

its own coin of gold before it lets go,

and press my palm against the ridged bark,

rough as a knuckle, warm where the sun

has been lying against it all morning.



Around me the whole canopy is spending itself —

red-rimmed leaves loosening from their stems

one at a time, unhurried, as if falling

were something practiced for years

before it was allowed to look this easy.

They drop through no wind I can name,

turning once, twice, a slow coin-flip

of red and gold, and land without a sound

in the grass already thick with yesterday's,

building a floor the color of embers

that have decided not to go out.



Then the ground answers before my ears do —

a weight moving through the undergrowth,

patient, deliberate, each step

pressed into the leaf-mold and held there

a moment before the next.

Antlers come first, parting the low light

into pieces the way a hand parts water,

and behind them a body the color of the bark

I've just been touching, steam lifting

off its back into the cold air, unraveling

the same way the leaves are unraveling,

as if the whole clearing were breathing

through a hundred small openings at once.



More come behind the first — necks bowed,

hooves finding the same worn path,

breath rising white from wide black nostrils

and hanging there before the branches take it.

One bull stops at the stream and drinks,

throat working, water darkening his chin,

the surface breaking into rings

that carry all the way to the bank

where I am kneeling, not breathing,

watching the rings reach the grass

and disappear into it, the way the leaves

keep disappearing into the ground,

the way the light keeps disappearing

into the animals' backs and coming out changed,

softer, the color of something remembered

rather than seen.



He lifts his head. Water drips

from his jaw in a string of small bright falls.

He crosses without hurrying,

without checking behind him,

as if the crossing cost him nothing,

as if the whole meadow had already

agreed to let him through.



Kneel where something larger than your name walks past.

Let the leaves land on your shoulders and stay there.

Do not stand until the herd is gone

and the light, too, has moved on without you.
