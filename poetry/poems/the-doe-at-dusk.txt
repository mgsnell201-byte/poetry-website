The Doe at Dusk

You’re walking the dog, leash slack in your hand,

when you see her, a doe, stepping out from the pines,

her fawn trailing like a shy footnote.

The light is doing that thing it does in late summer,

smearing gold across the field,

as if the sun forgot its lines and just gave up.



She doesn’t bolt, this doe, though her ears twitch,

catching the hum of your dog’s low growl,

the kind he saves for squirrels and shadows.

Her eyes, dark as the bottom of a well,

hold you there, pinned to the grass,

while the fawn nibbles a clover patch, oblivious.



It’s not like you expected this, you know,

this quiet scene unfolding like a page

from some book you meant to read but didn’t.

The air smells of cut hay and something older,

maybe the earth’s own breath,

stirring up thoughts you forgot you had.



You wonder if she’s forgotten her own fear,

standing there, legs thin as reeds,

while the fawn stumbles, all knees and curiosity.

The dog tugs, eager for the next scent,

but you stay, letting the moment stretch,

a thread of wonder pulled taut against the dusk.



And isn’t it strange, you think,

how the world keeps offering these gifts—

a doe, a fawn, a fading sky—

when all you did was step out the door

with a plastic bag and a leash,

expecting nothing but the usual walk home?
