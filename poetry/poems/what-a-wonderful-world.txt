What a Wonderful World

Everyone thinks Louis Armstrong was singing

about some brighter future we haven't reached yet,

a paradise just around the corner

where roses bloom redder and skies stretch bluer

than anything we've managed to produce so far.



But listen again to that gravelly voice—

he's not making a promise or laying out a blueprint.

He's taking inventory. Trees of green,

red roses too—present tense, every one of them.

He sees them for you and me, not for you and me later.



So this morning I'm trying it his way,

cataloging what's already here

instead of scrolling toward some shinier version:

the coffee maker's patient gurgle,

the refrigerator's one-note lullaby humming through the night

so I can pour cold milk at dawn,

the shower that answers when I turn the silver handle—

hot water, steady and sure.



The subway car where a dozen different faces

sit reading, sleeping, lost in private music,

each headed somewhere small and necessary—

a job, a visit, home—

and no one demands anyone leave.



The buildings stacked outside the window

like quiet promises we've somehow kept:

shelter against the cold, heat when we need it,

light at the flip of a switch.



Armstrong wasn't singing about tomorrow.

He was singing about right now—

this improbable constellation of ordinary miracles

we keep mistaking for a waiting room.



A better world?

It's not some distant upgrade we have to invent.

It's learning to see the one that's already working—

the green trees and red roses blooming today,

the strangers sharing space without strife,

the small engines of comfort that run while we sleep.



Look closer. Listen deeper.

Say it with that gravelly wonder:

What a wonderful world.

It has been the destination all along.
