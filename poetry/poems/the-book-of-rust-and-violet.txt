The Book of Rust and Violet

She finds it where the lathe
forgets its name,
wedged between studs,
leather swollen dark as a horse’s flank
after hard rain.
Mold blooms in gray constellations.
The brass clasp holds its one tooth
bitten down on forty years of dust.

She opens it not with eyes
but with the blade of her thumbnail,
prizing the first page
where the ink has eaten clean through,
river-water through limestone.

His father’s father—
the one they erased from the Bible—
had already walked west
with a sack of stolen seed,
the unexpected journey
like a fever in the blood.
So this one, her great-grandfather,
only followed the path
that had no markers.
He walked out of the war,
boots heavy with someone else’s blood,
and kept walking
until the rifle grew roots,
until the bayonet curved
into a scythe for cutting hay.

A pressed violet falls—
still purple, impossibly—
from the entry dated August, 1918.
He stole it from a garden
behind the lines,
carried it not for a lover
but because mercy,
he writes,
is the only ammunition
that multiplies when spent.

She reads how he ferried
the wounded boy in gray
across a wheat field at dusk,
the stalks closing behind them
like a seam in gold satin.
How he learned to listen
not to the god of his fathers
but to the foxfire burning
on the rotting logs—
a light that demands nothing,
only shows the next three feet
of dark forest.

In the margin,
a sketch of her own jaw,
the notch beneath the ear,
drawn decades before she was
even a pinch of salt
in anyone’s palm.
The secret is not scandal.
It is wildness.
The book does not say he was good.
It says he was free.

Her kitchen seems small now,
the refrigerator humming
its one gray note.
She closes the cover,
but the foxfire stays
in the hollow of her collarbone,
a blue-green lamp
for the journey
she has not yet told anyone
she is taking.
