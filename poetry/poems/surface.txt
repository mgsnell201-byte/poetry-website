Surface

It began in Latin:
super, above,
facies, face.
The upper face of a thing,
the side that turns toward you
when you arrive.

French softened it
and passed it across the water,
and English kept it
the way you keep a stone
from a beach you've forgotten,
smooth and ordinary,
worn by so much handling
that no one thinks to ask
whose face it was.

Lay your palm flat on the kitchen table.
This is surface:
oak grain under varnish,
the pale ring from last winter's coffee,
a scratch where a fork slipped
in a child's impatient hand.
It holds your plate, your elbows, your bills,
your head on the bad nights.

And yet physics says
you have never really touched it.
Your hand and the wood
repel each other a hair's breadth apart,
so every hand you have ever held
was a closeness, never a meeting.

Walk down to the lake at dawn.
A water strider stands on its skin
as if the water had agreed,
just this once,
to be a floor

Then there's the other surface,
the one that moves.

A problem surfaces in a meeting
after months of nodding heads.
A memory surfaces at three a.m.,
uninvited, dripping.
Something unspoken in a marriage
circles for years below the waterline
until one evening it breaks into the room.
A diver surfaces, lungs on fire,
and gulps the sky.

To surface is to rise from below
and become a face.

We say superficial as an insult,
as if the surface were the opposite of depth,
a thin lie stretched over the real thing.

But go back to the lake.

The water's skin holds the strider
because the molecules at the top
have nothing above them to hold,
so they hold harder
to everything beside and beneath them.

That is what a surface is:
the place where the depth,
with nowhere left to go,
pulls itself together
into something that can be touched.

So when the hard thing finally surfaces,
across a kitchen table,
in the face of someone you love,
don't call it shallow.

It is the whole deep weight of them
holding on,
rising to meet the air,
so that for once
it can hold you up.
