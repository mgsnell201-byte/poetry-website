The Morning I Became Myopic

I woke up squinting at the bedroom ceiling
which had become a pleasant blur of white,
the light fixture a soft explosion of brightness
like a small sun deciding to take up residence overhead.

Downstairs, I poured orange juice into what I hoped
was a glass and not the sugar bowl,
navigated by memory and faith toward the coffee maker,
that dark, humming altar I've worshipped at for years.

The newspaper had transformed into abstract art—
black marks dancing across white space
in patterns that suggested meaning
the way clouds suggest ships or the faces of old lovers.

I thought of Mr. Magoo, that cheerful catastrophe,
mistaking a bear for his nephew Waldo,
walking through construction sites as if they were parks,
his confidence inversely proportional to his vision.

And here I was, his philosophical cousin,
feeling my way through the familiar rooms,
each doorway a small act of faith,
each step a tiny leap into the probably safe.

There's something liberating, I'll admit,
about the world losing its sharp edges,
the way a face across the room becomes
just the idea of a face, a suggestion of features.

Maybe this is how we should approach everything—
the future, the past, the contents of our own hearts—
with Magoo's unshakeable optimism,
squinting at the blur and calling it beautiful,

walking forward with arms outstretched,
assuming the best about what we cannot quite see,
trusting that the bear is actually Waldo,
that the cliff is actually a curb, that the world
will catch us before we fall too far.
