The Unwitnessed World

I have stood at the edge of the pond

and watched the great blue heron

fold herself into the last light,



and I have stood there after,

when she was gone from my seeing

but not from the world.



I do not know where she wades

in the dark, what her yellow eye

finds in the water I cannot read.



But her slate wings are somewhere

angled above some shallow place,

her long neck coiled, patient,



as she has always been patient,

with or without me watching.



Meanwhile, in the nest above the marsh,

inside the pale blue shell no larger

than my closed fist,



a heron is becoming —

its gray down pressed

against an interior dark,



its hinged legs folded impossibly small,

its beak already the shape

of a life it has not yet begun.



I did not know this was happening.

It happened anyway.



And the star I found last night

with my one bare eye,

steadying myself against the cold —



that light left its source

before my grandmother's grandmother

drew her first breath.



The star may be ash now.

It may have fallen into itself

in a silence I have no word for.



And still the light comes.

Still it crosses all that emptiness

and arrives.



Tell me: what is the measure

of a thing that burns for a thousand years

and asks nothing of your seeing?



What would it mean to trust

that the world is full —

even when your eyes find only dark?
