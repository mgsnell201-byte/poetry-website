Dr. Chen's Practice

I want to tell you about Dr. Chen

who spends his mornings smoothing out wrinkles

on dogs whose ancestors were bred to have them.



It's a strange calling, I'll admit,

like being a professional eraser of history,

a reverse sculptor working against the original design.



The Shar-Pei puppies arrive with their excess skin

folded like expensive linen napkins

or those accordion files we keep in closets,



and Dr. Chen, with his steady hands and tiny scissors,

removes what centuries of Chinese breeders

so carefully cultivated—the folds that made them



look like worried philosophers, like old men

contemplating the nature of existence

while waiting for their dinner.



You have to wonder what the dogs think,

waking up from anesthesia with their new faces,

smooth as river stones, tight as drum skins.



Do they feel lighter? More aerodynamic?

Or do they miss the weight of all those wrinkles,

the way we might miss an old coat



we've finally thrown away, the pockets still holding

the shape of our hands, the collar remembering

the particular angle of our necks?



Dr. Chen tells me it's for their health—

the folds trap moisture, breed infection,

obscure their vision like heavy curtains.



And I believe him. I do.

But sometimes I think about those dogs

walking out of his office into the afternoon light,



their faces unfamiliar even to themselves,

wondering if beauty is always this practical,

this kind, this absolutely necessary.
