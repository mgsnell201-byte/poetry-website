The Joke Returned

I started my teaching day like all the others— jogging the track as a warm-up to the classroom. Johnny was special needs and wasn't interested in jogging, or any other form of exercise, except walking. So I walked backward in front of him, wiggling my fingers at his belly, quick and hopeful, trying to make his body want to run. He did not run. His legs, thin and angled like a grasshopper's pale limbs, moved at their own pace— which was not my pace, which was his. "Today I will reach him," I thought. "If I am foolish enough, if I am silly enough, he will follow." He did not follow. The track went on being a track. The morning opened its doors to everyone the same way— the ones who ran, the ones who walked, the ones who watched from the field with eyes that carried more than anyone had asked them to carry. Later, on the floor, our legs folded like two insects resting, his hand moved. Quiet. Quick. Deliberate. It slipped beneath my arm and pressed against my ribs— and there it was: the joke I had offered him, returned. Not on my schedule. On his. What do we know about waiting? What do we know about those who do not speak but listen with their whole bodies— who do not run, but arrive? I wanted to teach him something. He was teaching me the whole time: that patience is not a wall but a door, that the ones we try so hard to reach have already reached us and are simply waiting for us to notice. So tell me— what will you do with your one wild and listening life, when someone loves you in a language you have not yet learned to hear?
