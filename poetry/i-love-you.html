'I Love You'

You say I don't say it anymore, and this is true. The words have become like old keys I no longer reach for, hanging on their hook by the door. But I opened that door for you this morning before you could balance the bag of groceries against your hip, my hand already knowing the exact moment the weight would shift. Is this not a sentence? Yesterday, I asked about your day. Not while looking at my phone, not while moving toward the next thing, but standing in the kitchen's slant afternoon light, my face turned fully toward yours the way a plant turns toward sun— patient, attending, open. You told me about the meeting, the difficult email, the small victory no one else would think to ask about. I nodded. Such a simple motion, my head tipping forward, and yet I meant it to carry everything across the room that the room needed carried. Some nights I kiss you without preamble or reason, my mouth finding yours while you're reading, while you're washing your face, while you're standing at the window watching the last light drain from the oak's upper branches. These unasked-for gestures— are they not a kind of speech? The philosophers say language is what separates us from the animals, but I think of how the cat brings the injured bird to the doorstep, how the wolf touches its nose to the neck of its mate, and I wonder if we have it backward— if the truest communications are the ones that bypass the mouth entirely. I folded the laundry yesterday. This is such a small thing you may not have noticed. The clothes stacked by color, the socks matched and rolled, each article returned to its particular drawer. My hands moving through the warm cotton, making order, making care visible in a way that will be undone by evening and need doing again tomorrow. Isn't this the nature of devotion— the repetition that never asks to be noticed? I have stopped saying the words, yes. But watch: I bring you tea without your asking. I warm the car on cold mornings. I move your book from where you left it to the table beside your reading chair. I leave the light on when you work late. Every day, these small embassies I send across the border between us, carrying their wordless treaties, their quiet negotiations of tenderness. Perhaps what I mean when I don't say "I love you" is too large for three words anyway. Perhaps it needs a door held open, a question asked and answered, a mouth that finds another mouth simply because my body remembers what it wants to say and knows, finally, that saying and doing are not separate countries but two names for the same home.
