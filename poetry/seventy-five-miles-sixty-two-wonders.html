Seventy–Five Miles, Sixty–Two Wonders

At 7:05 a ladybug clocks in on the windowsill— tiny red suitcase already packed with black polka–dots and that is the beginning. Outside, the morning is mostly milk with a teaspoon of sun stirred in. A bus sighs. Clouds commute in slow white boots. We leave our street like a shy snail leaving its own perfect spiral of yesterday. The mailbox waves its red flag as if it knows we might not come back the same. I count the steps from porch to corner: one, two, three, until they become too many and melt into simple going. Journey is a number that refuses to sit still. By lunchtime we have traveled exactly 62 small astonishments: 1. A dog spelling its name in looping leash–curls. 2. A cracked egg of a cloud leaking light. 3. An ant parade hauling a crumb the size of Kansas. 4–13. Puddles pretending to be pieces of sky. 14–29. A swamp of summer air where frogs practice their burp–tenor scales. 30–47. Cornfields combing the wind’s hair straight back. 48–61. Roadkill mysteries, fencepost hawks, a billboard whispering EAT HERE to no one in particular. 62. The sudden thought that maybe my own bones are suitcases too. The bus hums its low song. My brother sleeps, mouth open, a small cave for dreams. I map raindrops on the glass, naming each drip a different future: this one astronaut, this one beekeeper, this one friend I haven’t met yet. Miles make us longer, I decide— not taller exactly, just more stretched between where we were and where we’re not yet. The ladybug reappears on my sleeve (same spots, different place). It walks my arm–road like a red caboose, losing track of the window, finding track on my skin. By late afternoon warm light leans heavy on barns and backs. We’ve collected so many new smells— diesel, dill pickles, wet gravel, old creek— the air feels crowded with invisible luggage. A sign announces: 75 MILES TO GO. Seventy–five, a staircase made of maybe. Each mile another question: Will the cousins still like us? Will the house remember my height on the wall? Will the river know my toes? The road unrolls, a long gray tongue tasting evening. Ditches deepen into blue. Cows become dark commas in the pasture, pausing the sentence of the day. We finally arrive where the map said we would, but my legs keep buzzing with leftover distance. Uncles appear with lemon–slice smiles. Aunties stack stories taller than suitcases. The river is exactly itself— cold, moss–mouthed, unimpressed with our odometer. And I think: A journey is not how far we go from home, but how many little strange things we agree to carry inside us— until one morning we wake up, open our eyes, and realize we ourselves have become the somewhere else someone is traveling toward.
