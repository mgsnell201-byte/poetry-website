The Claptrap Cabinet

In the dusty corner of my grandmother's attic stands A peculiar contraption she calls the Claptrap— Not filled with empty words or pompous plans, But literally designed to catch applause and snap Each clap between its wooden jaws— She built it during wartime rationing days When praise was scarce and approval laws Demanded we save every cheer that pays. The cabinet creaks with captured ovations: My first piano recital's nervous clapping, Uncle Ted's golf stories' false adorations, The polite applause when Cousin May was rapping. Some claps are genuine—bright and clean, Others hollow as campaign promises, A few are sarcastic (you know what I mean), And some are just social compromises. When loneliness visits on quiet nights, She opens the Claptrap's weathered door And releases a symphony of delights— Hands meeting hands from years before. The irony isn't lost on me, you see: Her Claptrap holds what claptrap lacks— Real moments of human harmony, Not empty words but authentic acts. So while the world spins tales of sound and fury, Signifying nothing in their verbose dance, Grandmother's wooden judge and jury Preserves each honest, fleeting chance When people chose to bring their palms together In genuine recognition of the moment— Her Claptrap hoards this precious weather Against the storm of false atonement. Maybe we all need such a device To separate the real from the performed, To hold what's honest, pure, and nice Before the world has it transformed Into the very thing its name suggests: Empty noise that masquerades as gold. But in that attic cabinet rests The truest story ever told.
