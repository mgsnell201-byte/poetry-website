Behind the Waterfall

As I looked at this photograph, it reminded me of the small cave I found last summer behind a waterfall in Yosemite. How I had to wade through knee-deep water and duck under a curtain of falling white to discover this pocket of stillness carved out by centuries of patient erosion. It occurred to me, standing there with my back against the cool stone, that this might be what consciousness is like— a dry space behind the constant cascade of experience, a place where you can stand and watch the torrent without being swept away, which sounds more philosophical than I intended, but there I was, soaked to the waist, thinking about Plato and his cave, wondering if he'd ever actually stood in one. The funny thing about being behind a waterfall is how the world looks when filtered through all that moving water— blurred, softer somehow, as if someone had smudged the edges of reality with their thumb. I could see people on the other side, just shapes really, walking along the path, taking their selfies, checking their phones, living their lives in the bright noise of the ordinary world, while I stood hidden in this chamber of mist and echo, neither here nor there exactly, a witness with wet shoes and a growing sense that I'd stumbled into some kind of metaphor I wasn't quite equipped to handle. You spend your whole life on one side of the cascade, don't you? Moving through the days, getting things done, checking items off lists, and then one day you duck behind the curtain and realize you've been looking at everything backwards— or maybe forwards for the first time— and the sound of all that falling water is actually quite beautiful from this side, this quiet side, where you can see how temporary it all is, how provisional, how the light breaks apart in the spray and reforms into something you could almost mistake for meaning. I stayed there longer than I should have, long enough that my legs went numb with cold, long enough to understand why hermits choose caves, why mystics seek out these liminal spaces between the world and whatever's behind it. But eventually you have to step back through, don't you? Back into the noise and motion, back to the parking lot and the drive home, carrying with you this small sadness— not quite grief, more like nostalgia for a place you only just discovered, knowing you'll never see things quite the same way again, now that you've stood behind the veil and looked out.
