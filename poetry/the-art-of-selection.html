The Art of Selection

I was drifting through one of those gallery spaces where the floors are so white and polished you feel like you’re walking on the surface of a frozen lake, careful not to leave a scuff or a stray thought behind. I was looking at the usual suspects— a field of blue squares, a wire sculpture of a chair— when I stopped in front of a small, rectangle canvas that featured nothing but a single, glowing orange. It wasn't sitting in a wooden bowl or resting on a checked tablecloth next to a knife. The artist had decided to pin it right in the middle of a deep, bruised violet sky, hanging there without the help of a branch or the laws of planetary motion. It was a ridiculous sight, really, like a piece of fruit that had suddenly developed ambitions of becoming a celestial body. But the more I looked, the more the room seemed to lose its temperature. The orange wasn't just a citrus; it was a tiny, pulp-filled sun, radiating a warmth that the white walls were trying their best to ignore. I thought about how a real sunset is always in such a desperate hurry to leave, tucking its colors away before you can even find the right words to describe the shade of pink. And I thought about the oranges in my kitchen, how they move quietly from the fruit fly stage to the blue-mold stage while my back is turned. But here, the light was held captive by the oil. Something in the pigment held the hour the way a room holds warmth after everyone has left. I stood there for a long time in my heavy coat, just warming my hands by the painted fruit, grateful for a beauty that was staying put, even if it was only doing so because it didn't have any other choice.
