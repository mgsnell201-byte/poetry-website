What the Morning Gives

I went out early, before the kettle, before the news, while the fog still lay in the low field like something the night forgot to take with it. Between two fence posts the spider had been working in the dark. Every thread held its row of dew, and when the sun came over the hill it went into each small drop and came out again as light. I watched one bead grow heavy, tremble, and let go. It fell onto a maple leaf below, a red one, the red of plums, already loosened from its branch, and the wind, which had been waiting for exactly this, lifted the leaf and the drop together and carried them down the slope toward the creek. So I followed. What else was I going to do with a morning like that? The creek was talking to itself the way it always does, low and patient, over the stones. The maples along its banks were burning, scarlet and amber and gold, letting go of their leaves one at a time as if each one were a small gift they had been saving all year. My red leaf rode among them, turning slowly, the dewdrop still shining at its center. A heron stood in the shallows, gray as the fog that had lifted, so still I almost walked past. Then, overhead, the geese, a long unsteady V of them, calling to one another, heading south, heading home, and the heron did not even look up. It already knew where it belonged. Where the creek bends near the road, a child was crouched at the water's edge, her mother waiting a few steps back. She was watching the leaves go by the way you'd watch a parade, and when my red one came floating past she reached for it and missed and laughed anyway. That was when the butterfly came, a monarch, late and a little tattered, going the same way the geese had gone, south, toward some warm country it had never seen and somehow already knew. It dropped down to rest on a stalk of purple aster right beside her hand. She went completely still. Her whole face opened like a door. She did not know its name. She did not need to. She only breathed, Oh, which is, I think, the oldest prayer there is. Then it lifted, and was gone, and she turned and looked at me, a stranger on the bank, to make sure I had seen it too. I had. I nodded. That was all, and it was everything. By evening I came back the way I had come. The fog was rising again from the low field. The geese were somewhere far down the sky. And the web was still there between the fence posts, dry now, nearly invisible, until the last light caught it and every thread turned gold. So this is my thanks, not for anything I earned, but for the web that let the drop go, and the leaf that carried it, and the creek that kept it moving, and the heron, and the geese, and the child, and the wings, everything traveling, everything held, everything handing the day to the next thing, the way it was handed to me. And what else is gratitude, after all, but this: to follow where the morning leads, to stand still in the wet grass, to say yes, and yes again, and thank you.
