The Falling

Have you seen them— the starfish in the tide pools, their five arms reaching like small suns pressed against the rocks? I used to think they were only creatures of the sea, born there, living their whole lives in the cold grip of the Pacific. But last night I stood on the beach watching the sky, and one star— I swear this is true— loosened itself from Cassiopeia and fell, not burning, not streaking across the darkness like the meteors do, but drifting, the way a leaf falls through October air, or the way a tired bird finally descends. It fell into the water without a sound. This morning I went back to the tide pools and there were more of them, the starfish, dozens clinging to the stones, their bodies the exact color of rust, of old light, of something that has traveled a long way to arrive here. I knelt down. I put my hand into the cold water and touched one. It didn't move. It held on to its rock with the patience of something that has learned a new way to shine. Tell me, what do you think happens to light when it grows weary? Where do you think the burning goes when a star decides it has illuminated enough of the emptiness? I think it comes here. I think it falls into the ocean and learns to crawl, to cling, to eat the small mussels, to live without fire. I think the sky is full of tired light and the sea is full of fallen stars and we, walking along the shore, are walking through a graveyard and a nursery at once— all those extinguished suns finding their way to a second life, smaller, slower, closer to the earth, but still here, still holding on, still reaching their arms out into the world.
