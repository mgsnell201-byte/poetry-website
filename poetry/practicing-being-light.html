Practicing Being Light

I have been looking out the kitchen window again, the one over the sink with the chipped white frame, and I hope you won’t mind if this time I imagine myself as a hummingbird. Because this morning I woke up heavy again, my thoughts thick as oatmeal, and I wanted to be something that couldn't carry much— no briefcase of worry, no pockets full of ought-to, just enough weight to be real, just light enough to leave. One second a man in his slippers, the next a small, green fever in the air, a jittering jewel headed for the fuchsia, those pink paper lanterns that line the porch, then over to the red salvia— flames frozen on their stems— and down to where the lawn relaxes into a soft, low meadow, each blade beaded with morning, the whole world smelling like it has just washed its face. From this angle, hovering over the columbine’s purple cups, you see how generous the flowers are— how they keep opening and opening, how the air itself seems to ring a little as your wings hum against it, a tiny engine of vowels and consonants, whirr and blur, the b and r of bright, brief, breath. Flying, it turns out, does not feel like flying in your dreams— no slow, solemn lifting over the town— but more like being the nervous thought that flits from one good idea to the next, never quite landing, just touching each thing lightly— this pink, that gold, this small blue bell of morning glory swinging faintly as you sip. And then there is the other part of the job, the night shift they don’t mention on the nature calendar, right between “migrate” and “mate.” You pick a twig, a thin, unimpressive one, and you sit down on it the way you might sit on the edge of a motel bed, and slowly you let the day drain out of you— the sugar, the color, the quick silver of your own attention— until your temperature drops, your heart, that frantic drummer, trades its sticks for felt mallets, and you cool into a tiny, feathered stone hanging from a branch. They call it torpor, which sounds a little like a sleepy torpedo, but really it is just rehearsal, a nightly practice of almost leaving. And maybe that is why, back at the sink with the water running, I feel a small pang for this creature who spends the bright, brief hours scribbling his name on the air in loops of invisible ink, then hangs himself quietly in the dark, trusting the cold, quiet universe to remember, by morning, how to turn him on again— the way you and I, who are not nearly as light, still lie down each night in our larger, heavier beds and hope for the same unreasonable mercy.
