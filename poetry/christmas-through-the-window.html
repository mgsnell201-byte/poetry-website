Christmas Through the Window

I have been watching the children press their noses against the cold glass of December, their breath fogging up small circles of wonder where they draw stars with their fingertips. You know the look—that particular widening of eyes when they see the lights strung along the eaves, as if each bulb contains a separate universe, a tiny captured sun they could hold in their palms. Inside a child's mind, Santa Claus is as real as gravity or toast, and the tree in the living room is not merely decorated but enchanted, a portal standing in the corner by the couch. Meanwhile, we adults are busy performing our own kind of magic, the sleight of hand that turns credit card statements into joy, the vanishing act of staying awake past midnight to assemble toys with instructions written by someone who clearly hated us personally. There's a tenderness in our exhaustion, a strange ache when we remember being the ones with our noses pressed to glass, back when December was a month-long held breath, when time moved like cold honey, and Christmas morning was the only morning that mattered in the entire history of mornings since the beginning of time. Now we are the ones hiding presents in the attic, eating the cookies left out for someone else, writing "From Santa" in handwriting we try to disguise, as if our children were handwriting analysts working for the FBI of shattered illusions. But here's the thing about Christmas— it asks us to believe again, not in flying reindeer or men in red suits, but in the architecture of attention, the way a family can gather in the glow of colored lights and become, for a moment, exactly what they hope to be: The child amazed by ribbon and paper, the teenager pretending not to care but caring deeply, the parents trading glances over coffee cups, speaking a language of knowing looks and small nods, the grandparents watching it all like filmgoers who've seen this movie before but still tear up at their favorite scenes. We string these moments together like lights on a wire, bright and fragile, plugged into something larger than any of us can name—not magic exactly, not religion precisely, but that rare frequency where wonder and memory and hope hum together in the key of cinnamon and pine, and for once, we all agree to leave the ordinary world standing outside in the cold while we huddle together in the warm conspiracy of December, pretending we don't know that the magic is just us, loving each other the best way we know how, which, if you think about it, is the most astonishing trick of all.
