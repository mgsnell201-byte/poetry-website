Have you seen them, the ancient ones, circling each other across the torn sky, their wings and hooves writing stories in the language of thunder and light? For a thousand years they have been dancing this way—the griffin's talons carving new valleys, the unicorn's horn splitting mountains like ripe fruit. Watch how they meet above the meadow, how their fury shakes the earth until flowers forget their names and rivers run backwards from the sea. The old woman in the village says they fight over a jewel of pure starlight, but I have been watching, listening to the spaces between their roars. See how the griffin's claws never quite find their mark, how the unicorn's charge always falls just short of the heart— this is not war, but conversation. They are teaching the world how to change, how mountains must sometimes break open to let new springs flow, how the sky needs tearing to let the light through. What do you call two immortal beings who have forgotten how to die, who make their own purpose from the raw clay of endless time? They are the world's memory of wildness, the reason children still believe in magic, the proof that some things are too beautiful to be tamed. Tonight they rest on opposite peaks, calling to each other in voices that sound like wind through ancient trees, like the first word ever spoken. Tell me—when you have lived forever, when you have seen every sunrise, what else is there to do but love so fiercely it reshapes the world?
