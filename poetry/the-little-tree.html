The Little Tree

She carried the seed in her pocket for three days before she found the right place to put it down, pressed it into the dark earth with her thumb, and began her watching. Every morning she came with her small watering can, tilting it just so, the way you might offer water to someone you loved who could not ask for it. When the rain fell hard, she held an umbrella, herself getting soaked through. When the sun blazed its indifferent fire, she made shade with her own body. And when winter came, the first winter, she wrapped the thin trunk— no thicker than her wrist—in an old blanket, tucking the edges as if putting a baby to sleep. But what do we know of the earth's wild rhythms when we are small? What do we understand of the necessary losses? In autumn the leaves began their bright dying, gold and orange and rust, and then the falling, the terrible falling. She stood beneath her tree and wept. She tried taping the leaves back on. She tried speaking to them. She didn't know that the tree was doing what trees do, that it was preparing, not perishing. Her mother found her there at dusk, kneeling in the leaf-litter, and knelt beside her. "Look," she said, and showed her daughter the buds already forming, tight and green, the tree's promise to itself. "It's making itself ready," she said. "It's making itself new." And the girl looked up into the bare branches, into the sky beyond, and saw—maybe for the first time— how beautiful the tree was in its starkness, how strong, how willing to let go of what it had been to become what it would be. How it trusted the world enough to stand there, naked and unafraid, waiting for spring.
