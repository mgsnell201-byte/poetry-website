The Kitchen That Wasn’t There

I was back in my mother's old kitchen, the one from before the remodel, before she died, before any of that— the yellow wallpaper with its faint tea stains, the smell of dish soap and Maxwell House. She was standing by the counter, maybe fifty, wearing that blue cardigan I'd forgotten about until right then, the one with the wooden buttons I used to spin when I was small enough to stand at her hip while she cooked. We were talking about something ordinary— whether it would rain, or if the Hendersons still had that loud dog—when she looked at me and said, as casually as you might mention the time, "You know I've been dead for three years now," and I said, "Yes, I know," the way you'd agree that it's Tuesday, or that the milk's gone bad. Then somehow we were folding towels together, the white ones with the blue stripes, standing side by side at the kitchen table the way we must have done a hundred times when such things were still possible. Neither of us spoke, but I could feel the warmth coming off her hands when we both reached for the same corner. When I opened my eyes, my bedroom seemed too bright, too insistent, the furniture all wrong somehow, refusing to be anything but real. I lay there trying to hold onto it— the yellow walls, the specific weight of those towels in my hands. I wanted to tell someone, but what would I say? That my dead mother and I folded laundry and neither of us found it strange? Now I'm sitting here with my coffee watching the morning arrange itself into the usual shapes and sounds, and all I can think about is how carefully we lined up those corners, how we both knew exactly what we were doing and never said a word about it.
