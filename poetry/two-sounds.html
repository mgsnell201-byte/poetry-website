Two Sounds

She learned to make cereal before she could read, standing on a chair to reach the box, the sound of Rice Krispies hitting the bowl the only conversation in the kitchen at dawn. Her mother on the couch where she'd been all night, needle on the coffee table catching morning light, and you might notice how still the woman looked, like a corpse arranged in the shape of sleep. You might think a child would cry or scream or beg for help, but she was practical even then— she knew the difference between sleep and stupor, knew which one allowed for questions and which did not. Her father came and went like violence, unpredictable and always damaging. When he was there, the air turned toxic, became the kind of atmosphere before a storm when animals grow nervous and birds stop singing and the sky turns the color of a bruise. You can almost hear their voices rising through the walls— her accusations, his denials, the sound of his hand meeting her face, which is sharper than you'd think, a crack like a bone breaking. She would sit on her bed with a book she pretended to read, her small finger frozen on a single word for an hour, listening to the architecture of their hatred, the blueprint of a marriage built on nothing but a bottle of whiskey and carelessness one August night when she was conceived but never wanted. They used to tell her she was unplanned, as if that explained the way they looked through her rather than at her, as if "accident" was another word for ghost. The night it ended, she was eight. She had made herself toast for dinner— butter and cinnamon sugar, which she thought was fancy— and she was sitting at the table when he came home. She knew the signs by then: the way he gripped the door frame to keep from falling, the vacant way his eyes found nothing, the heavy stumbling of his footsteps. Her mother was on the couch. Of course she was on the couch. She watched him cross the room, watched his hand go back, watched it come forward, and this time there was something else— the gun. Always in his jacket. Coming out like a terrible magic trick, the kind where everyone knows the ending but watches anyway because looking away would somehow make it more real. Two sounds. That's all it took. Two sounds and the smell of something burning that she later learned was gunpowder, and then a silence so absolute and so complete it felt like the world had stopped breathing. She doesn't remember calling anyone. She doesn't remember the police arriving or the neighbors or the questions or the blanket someone draped around her shoulders. She remembers the toast on her plate, how she'd only eaten half, how the butter had hardened into a small yellow pool, and thinking that she should finish it, that wasting food was wrong, that this was the kind of thing that mattered. Now when people ask about her childhood, she tells them it was quiet, which is the worst kind of lie because it's true. She leaves out the mornings making cereal on a chair, the nights listening through walls for the sound of her own death, the final evening when the silence finally became permanent. Some kinds of abandonment happen slowly, over years of not being seen or held or loved. Hers just happened to end in gunfire, which you might consider a mercy if you didn't know what came after. But she knows. She knows that sometimes, early in the morning, she pours herself a bowl of cereal and listens to the small sound it makes, and she's back in that kitchen again, a girl on a chair, learning that she would have to raise herself because no one else was coming, learning that love was something other children received, learning that survival was just another word for loneliness. And now, decades later, she still stands on chairs she doesn't need to stand on, still makes toast for dinner when the world feels too large, still flinches at the sound of footsteps in the hall, still carries that eight-year-old girl inside her like a stone, still wakes at dawn to silence, still waiting for someone to pull up a chair beside her and say, "You were not an accident. You were wanted. You are loved." But no one ever does. And she has learned, the way all abandoned children learn, that some hungers never get fed, that some wounds never close, that some rescues never come, and that the girl on the chair is still there, still eight years old, still standing, still alone.
