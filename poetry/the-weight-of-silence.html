The Weight of Silence

I pack my grandmother’s funeral program— not because I loved her most, but because I forgot to grieve her properly three years and three moves ago. Folded twice, it weighs nothing in my palm, everything in my chest. The photo shows her hands, not her face— hands that taught me to hold sorrow like water, cupped but never contained. Phoenix waits with its angry sun, subdivisions named after the cacti they paved over. I’ll rent a studio apartment where the walls are thin enough to hear my neighbors’ lives seeping through—their laughter a language I’ve forgotten, joy stacked like borrowed chairs. The program fits in my wallet next to a twenty-dollar bill and my expired California license— three paper rectangles measuring the density of what I’m carrying forward. At the new place, I’ll pin it to the refrigerator beside take-out menus and appointment cards, where it will yellow slowly under fluorescent light, softening each day until it crumbles into forgiveness— the heaviest thing I never learned to pack.
