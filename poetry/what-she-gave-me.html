What She Gave Me

I had been told coyotes were dangerous, which is true the way most true things are — partially, and not today. She was lying in the tall grass at the field's edge, one rear leg bent the wrong way, her tawny sides rising and falling with the careful economy of the badly hurt. I stood at the truck a moment longer than I needed to, checking the blanket in my hands, thinking about teeth. But when I walked toward her she lifted her amber eyes to mine and what was in them was not a threat — it was something closer to a question, the kind you ask when you have run out of other options and decided, for now, to trust. I covered her with the blanket. She did not growl. I lifted her — ten, maybe twelve pounds of heat and stillness — and she let me. All the way to the clinic she did not struggle. She lay in the crate the way the very sick lie, present and not present, here and somewhere else already. The vet met me at the door and I knew from his face before he said anything. Tell me — what do you do with the weight of a thing that chose you, briefly, at the end? She is in the grass somewhere now, or she is the grass, and I am still carrying the particular amber of those eyes, still learning what she was asking.
