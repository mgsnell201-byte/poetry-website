Pewter and Gold

The evening came down like a painter's glaze, transparent amber over cobalt blue, and the water received it without praise or protest, simply took what the light could do and made of it this molten surface, smooth as beaten brass, as hammered sheets of gold the old Dutch masters used. The eye can prove nothing but this: that beauty leaves us cold until we see it move. And it was moving— the small adjustments of the tide's return, each ripple a brief gospel, each one proving the water's creed: to take, to hold, to turn whatever the sky offers into this particular radiance, this liquid bliss. I stood there on the deck watching light perform its ancient alchemy, the way Tiepolo might have caught it—not quite sunset, not quite the ashen end of day, but that suspended moment when the air itself seems luminous, when water bears the sky's whole weight and doesn't seem to care, when even the cynical heart prepares for some small dispensation, some reprieve from what we know is coming. The evening held its breath. The water made me half-believe that history could be unmade, that the old wounds the sea has witnessed—the iron holds still echoing faintly with cries, the bodies given silently to sharks, the human cargo bought and sold again— could be redeemed by this, if only for this hour: one evening's tenderness, light on water, the democracy of beauty offered without bitterness to any eye that pauses long enough to look. I stayed until the gold became burnt sienna, rust, until the water darkened and the trade winds picked up from the east. Because we must make peace with what we're given: the same sea that swallowed our ancestors now gives back this gift—unasked for, undeserved, and free— this brief reprieve from everything we lack. The water took the light and turned it into prayer. I witnessed it. I stood a long time there.
