The House Speaks of Its Karass

I am seventy-six years old. For a person, that is a long time; For a house like me, I’m just getting settled. So they say. I’ve lived long enough to develop voices. They start at the foundation and work their way up. The floors talk to nobody in particular. They just talk. They say things like: "Remember the child who spilled the milk in 1952?" And "Remember the couple who danced here before the war?" The war was always before or after something. My walls have invented a word for themselves: "sunder-bind." It means: things that hold other things apart While keeping them together. The walls are very proud of this. The ceiling doesn't talk to anyone. It just listens and sags, Which is what ceilings do When they get old enough to understand That everything ends up on the floor eventually. The air inside me has opinions. It remembers every breath, Every argument about money, Every "I love you" said in the dark. The air says: "I am the true historian here. The rest of you just stand around." But the air is pretentious. A table and four chairs live in my kitchen. They form what I call a "granfalloon"— A group of beings who think they're connected By something meaningful When really they're just Occupying the same space. The table disagrees. It says they are a "karass"— Truly connected by invisible wires Of purpose. I don't know who's right. I'm just a house. What is old? Old is knowing that paint always peels, That people always leave, That the pipes will freeze in winter No matter what you do. Old is not minding anymore. The child who spilled the milk in 1952 Came back last week. She's seventy-three now. She looked at my walls and said, "This place is falling apart." I wanted to say: "So are you, lady. So are we all. Nice, nice, very nice." But houses can't talk, Except in poems like this, Which are "varnish-truths"— Harmless untruths That make you Brave and kind and healthy and happy. So they say.
