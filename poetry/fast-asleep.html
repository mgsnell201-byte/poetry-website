Fast Asleep

I have been thinking about the phrase "fast asleep" and how it makes no sense, as if sleep were a kind of sprinting, a race you could win by getting there first. My dog drops into sleep the way a stone drops into a pond— one moment she is watching me read, the next she is twitching in some meadow only she remembers, chasing what I'll never know. That's fast, I suppose. Meanwhile I lie in bed rehearsing a conversation from nine years ago, rearranging the furniture of a room I will never enter again, and sleep stands in the doorway with its coat on, checking its watch. No one ever says "fast awake", though that's the one that happens suddenly— the 3 a.m. bolt upright, the heart's insistence that something is wrong or was wrong once or will be. Slow asleep is what I know. The wood thrush pausing between phrases. The book sliding from my hand onto the blanket, onto the floor. And yet the fast sleepers— my dog, my daughter at six, the dead— they must be going somewhere in a hurry, arriving at the one country where the rest of us are still standing in line for a ticket, patting our pockets, not remembering which one holds the change.
