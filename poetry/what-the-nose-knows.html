What the Nose Knows

I have been thinking about the quilt again, the one folded in the cedar chest like a letter I keep meaning to answer. Someone stitched a wedding dress into the corner— all that ceremony reduced to a square, the vows hemmed in at last. Next to it, a child's painting of a horse that looks more like a table with ambitions, which is to say: accurate, in its way. Here is what I didn't expect: the mouse. Not the mouse of fairy tales, not the mouse of sympathy cards, but the actual mouse, nudging patches with the one thing it owns outright— that pink nose, damp and specific, a small lamp in a large dark. It doesn't know what a wedding is. It doesn't know what joy is, technically. And yet it pushes. Rearranges. Finds the seam between sorrow and satin and follows it, the way we follow a half-remembered melody into a room we thought we'd locked. The nose is the color of something I can't name without embarrassing myself. Sunset, maybe. The inside of a shell. The moment before you decide to say yes. I used to think the fragments meant failure— all those saved pieces, all that careful folding, the chest that holds what couldn't be kept whole. But watching the mouse in its small insistence I see something about tight spaces and the patient work done inside them. Its nudging isn't loss or ruin; it simply moves what is there. A quilt is not a wound. That pink, in the right darkness, is enough to steer by. What are you doing with your pieces? The ones in the chest. The ones you packed in tissue and forgot about, the ones that still smell like the original occasion. The mouse isn't asking— only acting. Yet that nose—a compass, a wick, a very small lantern— keeps pointing, as always, toward the thing you almost threw away.
