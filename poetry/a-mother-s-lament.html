A Mother's Lament

The toys are where they left them yesterday. A block half-built, a bear without its ear. The light comes in and doesn’t ask to stay— it moves across the wall, then disappears. I sit and watch the silence fill the space, not empty, but no longer quite the same. He used to hum before he knew the words. She used to laugh at things that weren’t a joke. They’d name the moon, then ask if it was mine, and I would lie, because that’s what we do. We give them myths to hold before they learn how much the truth can weigh when held too long. The floor still creaks beneath my careful step. I walk around the places they once fit. A crib becomes a shelf, a shelf a desk. The walls don’t mind—they’ve seen it all before. But I do. I remember every mark they made without intending it to last. There’s something in the way a child forgets that teaches us the art of letting go. They move ahead without a backward glance, while we stay back and name the things they dropped. A sock, a phrase, a drawing of a cloud— each one a kind of map we cannot read. I do not wish to hold them where they were. I only wish the echo stayed awhile. The way they said “tomorrow” like a place, or asked if dreams could happen while awake. I answered once. I don’t recall the words. But something in me still believes they’re true.
