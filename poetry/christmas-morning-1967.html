Christmas Morning, 1967

I want to tell you about the year my father woke us at five in the morning, not because he was excited about the gifts but because he wanted us to see the deer standing in our backyard, eating the apples that had fallen and frozen under the snow. We stumbled downstairs in our pajamas— my brother still half-asleep, my mother saying, "Bob, it's five in the morning," but going to the window anyway— and there they were: three of them, so close we could see their breath. My father said, "This is better than any present," and my mother said, "The turkey needs to go in by eight," and my brother said, "Can we open gifts now?" which is how every Christmas morning went in our house—all of us wanting something different from the same day. We opened presents after breakfast, which my mother insisted on, even though my brother and I would have survived on candy canes. My father always pretended he didn't know what was in the packages, even when he'd wrapped them himself. My mother cried every year at something small— a card, a scarf, once just the way the light came through the window. Now I do the same things with my own children. We eat at two o'clock because that's when my mother always served dinner. I make her stuffing recipe, which is really just Pepperidge Farm and a lot of butter. My daughter sets the table the way my mother taught her. My son asks every year if we can open gifts before breakfast, and every year I say, "After we eat." Some traditions are just stubbornness wearing a festive hat. But some— some are the way we stay connected to the people we've lost, the way we tell them we remember, we're still here, we're still doing it your way because it was a good way, because you taught us that Christmas isn't about the presents under the tree but about standing together at five in the morning watching deer eat frozen apples, about crying over small kindnesses, about getting up year after year and making the same stuffing, setting the same table, telling the same stories, which is its own kind of prayer, its own kind of attention, its own way of saying: this matters, you mattered, we are still a family, and this is how we know it— by gathering, by eating, by remembering, by standing at the window together and seeing what we can see.
