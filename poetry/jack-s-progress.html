Jack's Progress

The other day I was standing in the supermarket among the pumpkins piled like orange cannonballs, wondering who Jack was anyway, the one with the lantern we've been hearing about since we were small enough to believe that vegetables could protect us from ghosts. Turns out he was Irish, or the idea was, though back then they used turnips— those hard, bitter roots you'd need an hour and a strong arm to hollow out, just to wedge a coal inside and call it light. I suppose we should admire the commitment, carving a face into something that doesn't want one, all to ward off spirits who probably couldn't care less about your agricultural efforts, but I keep thinking about the first person who held up a turnip to the darkness and said, This will do, this will be enough. Then we got involved, naturally, and found the pumpkin waiting here like a gift, soft-fleshed and cooperative, practically begging to be scooped clean and given a grin the width of a dinner plate. Now you see them everywhere in October— on porches, in windows, lined up at school entrances with their identical triangular eyes, their LED candles that never burn out or down, safer, someone decided, than actual flame, as if we'd forgotten that the whole point was a little danger, a flicker that might fail. I wonder what old Jack would think, his name attached to this bright parade of vegetables smiling in the suburbs, their battery packs tucked in like secrets. Probably he'd shrug and light another turnip, one the size of his fist, the flame no bigger than a thumbnail, and walk off into the kind of dark we've learned to keep at bay with twenty watts and a timer, still believing, as he did, that a carved face means something when you can't see what's watching from the other side.
