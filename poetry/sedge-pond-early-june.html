Sedge Pond, Early June

I walk down through grass silvered with dew, before the sun has climbed high enough to dry it, my ankles numb inside their own chill, cattails leaning their brown heads over water gone thick with sky. Marsh wrens cut the air with small cries. A bullfrog drops from a low root into water that closes over him without a sound. I hold my hand out over the shallows, one finger lifted, still as the sedge, and wait while the sun climbs and my shoulder aches and the morning goes on without me, minute by minute, not caring if I stay. The dragonfly comes from nowhere, blue as chicory, quick as a thought turning, its double wings ticking like dry grass in a wind only it feels. Then it comes down. Six legs, thin as sedge stems, fold onto my finger. Its body, a blue stalk between two wings, its two dark seeds of eyes turn to take in the whole trembling pond, the whole sky, and me, and do not lift away. I do not breathe. My arm turns into a dead branch, holding still, not a boy's arm at all, and the dragonfly rides it, its wings ticking down to nothing, staying. You want the world to notice you— so stop moving. Stop asking. Empty your hand of everything you meant to do with it. The dragonfly lifts once, testing the air, then settles again on the same finger, its wings printing small circles of light onto the skin of the pond.
