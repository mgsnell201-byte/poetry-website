The Centipede in My Tea Cup

You know how sometimes you reach for a cup without looking, your hand already anticipating the smooth curve of porcelain, and then you glance down— There he is, all legs and segments, a miniature train of body parts curled at the bottom of what was supposed to be my vessel for morning tea. I counted once, or tried to, those pairs of legs rippling along his sides like tiny oars on a Roman galley, but lost track somewhere after twenty. The ancient Greeks would have made him a monster, given him a name that translates to "hundred-footed," though I suspect they too would have been lousy at the actual math. He seems unbothered by his predicament, content to patrol the white interior, a security guard making rounds in the world's smallest, emptiest arena. I think of all the cups I've drunk from— the cracked ones, the inherited ones, the one I bought in that shop in Maine twenty years ago on a trip I barely remember— and how none of them ever held anything quite like this, this breathing comma of chitin, this ambulatory ellipsis navigating the place where my lips were meant to go. So I carry him outside on the porch, watch him pour himself over the rim and disappear into the gap between two floorboards, then return to rinse the cup three times, maybe four, before filling it with Earl Grey, sitting down to wonder why we remember the interruptions more than the routine, why the centipede gets a poem while a thousand ordinary mornings slip away unnoticed, like water through a drain.
